A Treaty Defines Spanish Florida’s Northern Boundary

1795 — The Treaty of San Lorenzo settled a disputed frontier and changed access to the Mississippi River.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

A Florida Boundary Negotiated in Spain

On October 27, 1795, Thomas Pinckney and Manuel de Godoy signed the Treaty of San Lorenzo in Spain. Often called Pinckney’s Treaty, the agreement settled disputes between the United States and Spain over boundaries and navigation. For Florida, it helped define the northern limit of Spain’s East and West Florida. For Americans beyond the Appalachian Mountains, it addressed access to the Mississippi River and the port of New Orleans. [1]

The negotiations took place far from the land and waterways they affected. That distance is part of the story. Florida’s colonial boundaries were shaped by imperial policy, European warfare, American expansion, and trade. Understanding the peninsula’s past requires looking beyond the outline of the modern state and asking which governments claimed the wider region at different times.

Why the Frontier Was Disputed

The young United States and Spain disagreed about territory in areas now belonging to Alabama and Mississippi. Spain maintained positions in the disputed region and wanted to restrain American settlement and commerce near its possessions. American settlers and merchants, meanwhile, wanted greater freedom to use the routes leading from the interior to the sea. The arguments concerned both political control and practical access. [1]

A boundary was more than a line separating colors on a map. It could determine which officials exercised authority, where troops might remain, and which government claimed the right to regulate movement. For a person living near the disputed frontier, these questions could affect travel, trade, and the future security of a settlement.

Rivers added another complication. They were transportation routes as well as possible boundaries. A government controlling the lower reaches of a river could influence people living far upstream. New Orleans consequently mattered to communities that were nowhere near the coast. Goods moving down the Mississippi needed access to a port where they could be stored or transferred for a continuing voyage.

Why Spain Became Willing to Negotiate

Spain’s position changed during the wars of the French Revolution. Military setbacks and uncertainty about Britain and the United States created reasons to seek a settlement. President George Washington selected Pinckney as the American negotiator, while Godoy directed Spanish diplomacy. The agreement emerged from this changing balance rather than from a sudden disappearance of competing interests. [1]

The broader lesson is that a negotiation in one region can become possible because of events in another. Florida’s frontier was linked to decisions about alliances and security across the Atlantic. Officials weighed local territorial claims against the potential costs of a larger conflict. A concession that appeared undesirable earlier could become useful under different circumstances.

Pinckney’s assignment required turning that opportunity into written terms. An agreement had to address not only a general wish for friendship but the actual points that had produced tension. The treaty’s detailed articles show what that work involved: descriptions of boundaries, navigation rights, procedures, and obligations for both governments.

Drawing the Line in Words

Article II described the southern boundary of the United States against Spanish East and West Florida. It began at the Mississippi River on the thirty-first parallel, ran east to the Apalachicola or Chattahoochee river system, followed that system to its junction with the Flint, and continued toward the head of the St. Marys River and the Atlantic. The wording combined a line of latitude, rivers, and a connecting land boundary. [2]

That description did not place a visible mark across the countryside. Article III required commissioners and surveyors to run and mark the boundary, prepare plats, and keep journals. The treaty therefore supplied both an agreement and a process for implementing it. Diplomatic language had to be translated into work on the ground.

Surveying involved more than locating a convenient straight line. The officials needed to identify the features described in the agreement and create records both governments could recognize. Their maps and journals would help connect the written treaty to an actual landscape. The requirement for shared procedures reveals how easily a settlement could generate new disagreements if its implementation remained vague.

Navigation and the Right of Deposit

The treaty also secured navigation of the Mississippi for American citizens. Its provisions allowed Americans to deposit goods at New Orleans without the contested duties, subject to the treaty’s terms. This right of deposit mattered because a river voyage and an ocean voyage were often different stages of a commercial journey. A place to hold and transfer goods could be as important as permission to move along the river. [2]

The result linked frontier development with international trade. A farmer’s or merchant’s prospects depended partly on decisions made by governments controlling distant routes. Settled access reduced a major obstacle, even though an agreement could not remove every uncertainty from commerce. The treaty shows why transportation rights could carry political importance comparable to territory itself.

People Outside the Negotiating Room

The treaty’s benefits were not shared equally. Native nations occupied and used the lands affected by the agreement, but they were not equal negotiating parties to it. Article V addressed relations with Native communities through obligations made by Spain and the United States. Its language reveals the governments treating Indigenous affairs as part of their own diplomatic settlement. [2]

This perspective deserves scrutiny. A treaty described as a success for one nation could increase pressure on other peoples’ lands and weaken their options. Evaluating the agreement requires asking who gained an advantage and who had no comparable opportunity to consent. That question prevents an account of diplomatic achievement from becoming an assumption that expansion benefited everyone.

Why It Matters to Florida

A useful reading of the treaty follows its verbs as well as its geography. The parties agreed to withdraw, appoint, survey, record, and permit. Each action required people to do something after the negotiators finished. That is why a signed document should be treated as the beginning of implementation rather than proof that every difficulty immediately disappeared. The treaty supplied rules and commitments whose consequences unfolded through later decisions in the borderlands.

October 27 is the signature anniversary. Ratification and implementation followed, so the boundary did not become fully established through a single act of signing. The distinction connects Florida history to the practical work of diplomacy, surveying, and administration.

Pinckney’s Treaty helps explain why colonial Florida cannot be understood simply as today’s state under an older name. Its frontiers belonged to a much wider struggle over land and river access. The agreement also connects Florida to the Mississippi Valley and European politics. Following those connections reveals how a line negotiated in Spain could influence movement, power, and opportunity across the southeastern borderlands of North America.

Classroom Connection

Reading Prompt

Before reading: How could control of a distant river port affect a farmer living far inland?

Glossary

Treaty
A formal agreement between governments.
Ratification
Formal approval of an agreement through the required governmental process.
Parallel
A line of latitude running east and west.
Right of deposit
Permission to store goods at a port for transfer or onward shipment under agreed terms.

Reading Check

  1. Who signed the treaty on October 27, 1795?
  2. Why did the agreement require commissioners and surveyors?

Think and Discuss

Why can a treaty be advantageous to its signers while harmful to people outside the negotiations?

Short Writing Prompt

Use the boundary and river-access provisions to explain how the treaty connected geography with political power. Include one consequence for people not represented as equal negotiating parties.

Teacher Notes and Suggested Answers

Thomas Pinckney represented the United States and Manuel de Godoy represented Spain. Surveyors had to translate the written boundary into a marked line and records. Responses should explain trade access and acknowledge Native communities’ interests rather than assuming expansion benefited everyone.

Suggested use: introduce the reading prompt, read the account, check comprehension, then select the discussion or writing task. Adapt vocabulary and response length to the class.
