Tobacco Country: North Florida’s Forgotten Cash Crop

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

The Suwannee Valley’s Bright-Leaf Tobacco Economy

Opening day at the tobacco market decided whether a year of labor became a livable income.

On a summer morning in Live Oak, the sound that meant the year’s work had paid off was the chant of a tobacco auctioneer, fast and rhythmic and nonsense to an outsider. To farm families along the warehouse floor, watching golden leaf laid out for buyers, that chant was the most important sound of the year. Most people think of citrus, strawberries and sugarcane as Florida’s historic crops. Yet a stretch of the Suwannee Valley spent most of the twentieth century as flue-cured tobacco country.

Bright Leaf, Not Shade Leaf

Florida had two tobacco traditions. In Gadsden County near Quincy, growers began raising tobacco under cloth shade in 1896 to produce the thin leaf used to wrap premium cigars; shade production peaked in the mid-1960s and ended in the 1970s. The Suwannee Valley crop was different: flue-cured, or bright-leaf, tobacco grown in open fields for cigarettes, the same general type that built the Carolina tobacco economies, though on a smaller scale. Core counties included Suwannee, Hamilton, Columbia and Bradford, with auction warehouses in Live Oak, Jasper and Lake City. Sources differ on when flue-cured production took hold, from just before World War I to the 1920s. According to UF/IFAS, tobacco cash flow let growers diversify into cattle, swine and other crops and helped build many rural towns.

A Crop That Demanded Everything

Work began in spring with seedlings set out by hand, and continued through summer with suckering, topping and watching for hornworms. Harvest came in stages. Leaves ripen from the bottom up, so a field was primed, picked leaf by leaf, several times over several weeks. At the height of harvest an entire farm family, often with hired help, spent long days bent over in the rows with hands stained dark by tobacco gum.

The Tobacco Barn

Primed leaves were strung on sticks and hung in a tall, tightly sealed wooden barn to cure. Flue-curing carried heated air through metal flues so the leaf never met open flame or smoke. Someone tended the heat around the clock for the several days each cycle took: too fast and the leaf scorched, too slow and it rotted. Family members took turns through the night, and the smell of curing tobacco became a lasting memory for many who grew up around it.

The Auction Warehouse

Cured and graded leaf went to auction in large open warehouses where each farmer’s pile was laid out on the floor. Buyers judged color, texture and quality while the auctioneer moved from pile to pile in a rapid chant. Opening day was among the most important of the year, and a State Archives of Florida photograph records a master of ceremonies at the opening of the Jasper market on July 26, 1949. A published history of Columbia County reports about a million pounds of flue-cured tobacco produced in the greater Suwannee Valley by the mid-1930s.

Quotas and Decline

From 1938 until the 2004 crop year, according to the USDA Economic Research Service, a federal program assigned marketing quotas to farms and supported prices, giving many small farms stability. The decline came gradually. UF/IFAS cites high freight costs, lower-cost production abroad, high input costs and labor access, and says many producers sold out or switched crops. Growing awareness of tobacco’s health effects reduced demand. The quota program ended with the Fair and Equitable Tobacco Reform Act of October 2004, which paid quota owners $7 per pound of 2002 quota and producers $3 per pound of 2002 effective quota. UF/IFAS reports tobacco is now grown on just over 1,100 acres in the Suwannee Valley, with a farm gate value above $7 million. Many old barns stand empty or have fallen.

Quick Facts

Tobacco type

Flue-cured (bright leaf), for cigarettes

Contrast

Gadsden County’s shade-grown cigar wrapper, begun 1896

Core counties

Suwannee, Hamilton, Columbia and Bradford (UF/IFAS also lists Lafayette and Alachua)

Market towns

Live Oak, Jasper and Lake City

Curing

Heated barns with metal flues

Auction

Loose-leaf sales with a chanted call

Federal program

Quotas and price supports, 1938 through the 2004 crop year

Program buyout

Fair and Equitable Tobacco Reform Act, October 2004

Today

Just over 1,100 acres in the Suwannee Valley (UF/IFAS)

Did You Know?

  • Leaves ripen from the bottom of the plant up, so farmers picked each field several times.
  • Flue-curing kept leaf away from open flame and smoke.
  • The federal tobacco quota program lasted about 66 years.

Vocabulary

Flue-cured: dried with heated air carried through metal pipes called flues.

Priming: picking tobacco leaves a few at a time as they ripen.

Quota: a set limit on how much a farm may produce or sell.

Discussion Questions

1. Why did tobacco farming require so much hand labor from an entire family?

2. How did the federal quota program help small farms, and how did it limit them?
