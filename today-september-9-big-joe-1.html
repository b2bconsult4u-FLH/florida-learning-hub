Big Joe 1 Tests Mercury's Heat Shield

By Wm. E. McMullen II

September 9, 1959 — At 3:19 a.m., NASA launched the uncrewed Big Joe 1 Mercury spacecraft from Launch Complex 14 at Cape Canaveral. The mission was an early—and essential—step toward sending the first American astronauts into space.

Big Joe was not a fully operational spacecraft. It was an instrumented, full-scale model built to test Mercury's ablative heat shield, which was designed to protect an astronaut from the tremendous heat produced during atmospheric reentry. More than 100 temperature sensors were installed around the capsule and beneath its heat shield to record how the structure responded.

An Atlas 10-D rocket carried the capsule to an altitude of approximately 95 miles. During its 13-minute suborbital flight, Big Joe reached nearly 14,900 miles per hour and traveled about 1,500 miles downrange over the Atlantic Ocean.

The flight did not proceed exactly as planned. The Atlas rocket's two outer engines failed to detach after exhausting their fuel, leaving the vehicle burdened with additional weight. The capsule separated later than intended and landed about 500 miles short of its planned recovery area.

Despite the booster malfunction, the spacecraft itself performed successfully. The heat shield survived the high-speed return through the atmosphere, the capsule remained intact, and the destroyer USS Strong recovered it approximately seven hours after launch. Engineers were then able to examine the capsule and confirm that the basic heat-shield design could protect a Mercury spacecraft during reentry.

Big Joe demonstrated an important principle of early space exploration: a mission could experience serious problems and still produce the evidence needed to move forward. The test helped clear the way for later Mercury flights, including Alan Shepard's 1961 suborbital mission and John Glenn's 1962 orbital flight.

The original Big Joe capsule survives in the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum collection.
