Today in Florida History · September 5

Greater Miami Is Created

September 5, 1925 — Miami became a much larger city.

Three days earlier, voters had approved the annexation of Coconut Grove, Silver Bluff, Allapattah, Lemon City, Buena Vista, and Little River. On September 5, the expansion took effect and what contemporary accounts called “Greater Miami” was created.

It was more than a change on a municipal map.

Several communities that had developed their own identities—some long before Miami became a city—were now incorporated into a rapidly expanding Miami.

And the timing could hardly have been more appropriate. Florida was in the middle of one of the greatest real-estate booms in its history.

Miami Was Growing at Extraordinary Speed

Miami had incorporated only in 1896.

Less than thirty years later, South Florida was experiencing extraordinary population growth, construction and land speculation.

Railroads had connected the region with northern markets. Automobiles were changing how people traveled. Developers promoted Florida across the United States as a place of sunshine, opportunity and seemingly endless available land.

New subdivisions appeared. Hotels rose. Roads were constructed. Property changed hands at astonishing speed.

Miami was no longer simply a small tropical city at the mouth of the Miami River. It was becoming a metropolitan center.

And that growth increasingly collided with the boundaries of older neighboring communities.

Communities Older Than Greater Miami

The places Miami annexed were not empty land waiting for the city to arrive.

They were communities.

Coconut Grove, for example, had existed as a recognizable settlement long before Miami's incorporation. Its residents had voted to incorporate their own municipality in 1919.

Lemon City developed along Biscayne Bay north of early Miami.

Allapattah grew west of the city's original core.

Buena Vista and Little River developed northward as settlement spread along transportation routes.

Silver Bluff occupied land south of central Miami near Biscayne Bay.

Each had its own history.

By 1925, however, the explosive growth of Miami increasingly blurred the lines separating those communities from the city itself.

Why Create “Greater Miami”?

Growing cities require infrastructure.

Roads must connect. Water and sewer systems must expand. Police and fire protection must cover increasingly developed areas. Transportation networks must cross municipal boundaries.

Planning becomes more complicated when one continuous urban area is divided among several governments.

Supporters of annexation saw consolidation as a way to create a larger city capable of coordinating that growth.

But annexation also meant something else:

Independent communities would lose control over their own municipal governments.

That made the issue deeply local.

A line on a map might look insignificant from Tallahassee or Washington. To someone living in Coconut Grove or another affected community, that line could determine who governed the place they called home.

September 2: The Vote

On September 2, 1925, residents voted on the proposed expansion.

The proposal called for Miami to annex Coconut Grove, Silver Bluff, Allapattah, Lemon City, Buena Vista and Little River.

Three days later, on September 5, the expansion became official.

Greater Miami had been created.

The names of those earlier communities did not necessarily disappear. In fact, several remain familiar Miami neighborhood names a century later.

But their political relationship with the city had changed permanently.

A Photograph Taken That Very Day

Remarkably, Florida Memory preserves a photograph taken in Miami on September 5, 1925—the exact day Greater Miami was created.

Photographer William Arthur Fishbaugh documented construction of the Shoreland Arcade in the 100 block of East Flagler Street and Northeast First Street.

The photograph is almost a perfect visual companion to this story.

It does not show politicians signing documents or voters standing at ballot boxes.

It shows construction.

Miami was physically rebuilding itself at the same moment it was politically enlarging itself.

The Photograph Tells Its Own Story

Look closely at an image like this and the 1920s land boom becomes easier to understand.

Miami was not merely attracting new residents.

It was being built at tremendous speed.

Construction crews, commercial buildings, streets, automobiles, hotels and new developments were transforming what had been a comparatively small South Florida community only a generation earlier.

Fishbaugh was especially well positioned to document that transformation. He moved his photographic studio to Miami in 1920 and performed commercial photography for developers including Carl Fisher and George Merrick.

His photographs became a visual record of a city changing almost faster than cameras could document it.

The Boom Would Not Last

There is also considerable historical irony in the date.

Greater Miami was created near the height of the Florida land boom.

The optimism of 1925 would not continue indefinitely.

The speculative real-estate market began weakening. Transportation problems, financial difficulties and declining confidence helped bring the boom to an end.

Then, on September 18, 1926, a devastating hurricane struck the Miami area.

The storm destroyed buildings, damaged infrastructure and intensified the region's economic problems.

Florida's spectacular boom had collided with economic reality and natural disaster.

But the larger Miami created during the boom remained.

The Old Communities Survived in Their Names

Municipal annexation did not erase local identity.

That may be one of the most interesting legacies of September 5.

More than a century later, people still speak of Coconut Grove, Allapattah, Little River, Silver Bluff and Buena Vista.

Those names preserve pieces of the earlier South Florida landscape.

They remind us that large cities rarely appear all at once.

They grow by absorbing farms, settlements, towns, neighborhoods and communities that already have histories of their own.

Miami Before the Skyline

Today, Miami is internationally recognized for its skyline, beaches, tourism, finance, international trade and cultural connections throughout the Caribbean and Latin America.

That modern image can make it difficult to imagine how recently the city emerged.

Miami was only 29 years old as an incorporated city when Greater Miami was created.

The September 5 annexation therefore captures Florida during one of its recurring periods of extraordinary transformation.

Population was arriving. Land was being subdivided. Buildings were rising. Roads were spreading. And city boundaries were moving outward to keep pace.

On September 5, 1925, six surrounding communities became part of Miami.

The event helped create the geographic framework of the city that generations of Floridians would come to know.
