Stage Routes

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

Florida’s Scheduled Travel Network and the Stops That Connected the Territory

At every stop, information moved.

In a pioneer Florida settlement, word of the approaching stagecoach was news. It meant mail from relatives in Georgia, newspapers weeks old but still carrying a world of information, and passengers who might know what was happening beyond the settlement’s horizon. Stage routes were among the territory’s scheduled transportation services, moving mail and people along a defined route at a published time. They were not fast or comfortable, and not always reliable, but they made scattered communities feel connected.

The Federal Mail Contract

Stage service grew out of the federal mail. Historian Richard J. Stanaback, working from Postmaster General reports and Post Office records, found that Florida’s post roads grew from 121 miles in 1821 to 2,920 miles in 1845, with about fifty post offices by the latter date. A stagecoach was only one way the mail traveled; he also documents horseback riders, army mules, steamboats and packet ships. A stage carried Pensacola’s mail to Claiborne, Alabama, by 1821, and regular stage mail began at Tallahassee in 1827. Carriers were paid annual sums, such as $1,200 a year on the Pensacola–Claiborne route from 1823 to 1826, and the Post Office could fine them for failures.

The Stage Stop

A stage stop was where horses were changed, passengers ate and sometimes rested, and the mail was exchanged. The best evidence for a line’s stops is the operator’s own notice. A July 1855 broadside by Hubbard L. Hart, held by the State Archives of Florida, lists the stops on the Palatka-to-Tampa line as Orange Spring, Orange Lake, Ocala, Camp Izard, Augusta, Melendez, Pierceville and Fort Taylor. Florida Memory identifies Ocala and Melendez (now Brooksville) as overnight stops, where passengers lodged in hotels or boarding houses. The distances between stops are not given in the sources examined, so no spacing is claimed here.

Major Routes

Most routes followed roads that already existed. In 1824 Congress appropriated $20,000 for a road between Pensacola and St. Augustine; John Bellamy completed the St. Augustine–Tallahassee section in 1826, according to state historical markers. Whether mail on that road traveled by coach or by a rider on horseback is not established, and no date has been found for the first stagecoach there.

The best-documented stage line of the 1850s ran across the peninsula. Hart’s broadside announced his acquisition of the Palatka–Tampa line, with stages leaving each end on Mondays and Thursdays at 7 a.m. and arriving the following Wednesdays and Saturdays. At Palatka the line connected with steamboats, and at Tampa with steamers to New Orleans and Key West. Florida’s marker program records that a stage line carried mail and passengers over the Old Stage Road between Newnansville and Micanopy from 1866 to 1876.

War and the Mail

The Second Seminole War (1835–1842) was a federal military campaign in which the United States sought to remove Seminole and Miccosukee people from Florida by force. Seminole and Miccosukee people resisted, and their descendants live in Florida today. Stanaback reports that mail travel through the interior was disrupted, with attacks on carriers between 1835 and 1841.

The Stage and the Community

The stage put communities on a schedule, and mail day could be a social event. The record also shows the arrangement’s limits. Stanaback reports that schedules were often ignored, that stages overturned, and that papers sent from Washington in November 1822 reached St. Augustine in August 1823. Florida Memory notes that railroads made most stage routes obsolete after the Civil War. The Old Stage Road marker ties the decline of its line to steamer service in 1876, the arrival of the railroad at Micanopy in the early 1880s, and the abandonment of Newnansville by 1900.

Quick Facts

Post roads

121 miles in 1821; 2,920 miles in 1845; about fifty post offices by 1845 (Stanaback)

Early stage mail

Pensacola–Claiborne by 1821; Tallahassee in 1827 (Stanaback)

Bellamy Road

Congress appropriated $20,000 in 1824; completed 1826 (state markers)

Hart’s Palatka–Tampa line

Announced July 1855; stages left each end Monday and Thursday at 7 a.m. (State Archives broadside)

Contract payments

Annual sums, e.g., $1,200 a year on Pensacola–Claiborne, 1823–1826 (Stanaback)

Old Stage Road line

Newnansville–Micanopy, 1866–1876 (state marker)

Stage fares

Not established; no fare schedule located

Stop spacing

Not established; no mileages located

End of the era

Varied by region; most routes obsolete after the Civil War (Florida Memory)

Vocabulary

Stage route: a regular, scheduled coach route between towns.

Relay station: a stop where tired horses are exchanged for fresh ones.

Contract: a formal agreement to do a job for an agreed payment.
