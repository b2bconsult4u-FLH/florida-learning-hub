River Travel

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

Florida’s Rivers Were Its First Highways — and the People Who Used Them Knew Every Bend

Florida’s rivers were its first highways, requiring no construction and no maintenance.

Before the military road builders arrived with their axes, Florida already had a highway system. It had been in use for thousands of years, and it was the rivers. Florida’s flat terrain, which made road building so difficult, made river travel comparatively easy, since the current on most rivers was gentle enough to pole or paddle upstream. Native peoples used Florida’s waterways for thousands of years, and pioneer families added their own chapter.

Florida’s River Network

The St. Johns, Florida’s longest river, is one of the few in the United States that flows north. According to the St. Johns River Water Management District, it rises in the marshes south of Melbourne and runs about 310 miles before reaching the Atlantic at Mayport. On the Gulf side, the Hillsborough ran to Tampa Bay and the Peace to Charlotte Harbor. In North Florida the Suwannee, Apalachicola, Choctawhatchee and Escambia carried cargo toward the Gulf. The Apalachicola drew on a basin reaching well into Georgia and Alabama.

The Boats

The dugout canoe, cut from a single cypress log, was the most basic and widely used craft; its design had been refined over centuries by Native peoples. The bateau, flat-bottomed with squared ends, could be built quickly from sawn planks and carried heavy cargo such as cotton bales, turpentine barrels and hides in shallow water. Going upstream meant poling, among the most demanding labor of the river trade. Steamboats later joined these craft, a story told in FLH-0170, and no start date for steamboat service on the St. Johns is asserted here.

Spring-Fed Rivers

Rivers fed by the limestone springs of the north-central peninsula, such as the Ichetucknee, Silver and Rainbow, offered clear, dependable water. Glass-bottom boats were carrying tourists on the Silver River by the late 1870s, according to the attraction’s own history. The rivers were also larders: a family with a dugout, a throw net and a gig had a ready source of fish.

Highways of Commerce

Cotton from North Florida and from the Chattahoochee and Flint basin moved down the Apalachicola to the port of Apalachicola, which a local museum describes as the third largest cotton-shipping port on the Gulf of Mexico. Cattle bound for Cuba did not float; they were driven overland to Punta Rassa at the mouth of the Caloosahatchee. At Cedar Key, cedar logs fed the pencil mills that, according to Florida State Parks, first opened in 1865.

Hazards and Community

The rivers were not safe highways. Submerged logs could stave in a loaded bateau, alligators made capsizing a serious emergency, and summer thunderstorms left boaters with no shelter. Fever was a constant danger in low, wet river margins. Even so, river landings became gathering points for settlements, and a family on a navigable river could often reach a neighbor’s landing faster by canoe than by sand road.

What Remains

As roads and railroads made water transport largely obsolete, Florida’s rivers became recreational waterways in the twentieth century. The Suwannee, the St. Johns and the spring-fed rivers still run, and paddlers on them can still imagine what early travelers heard when the river was the road.

Quick Facts

Principal craft

Cypress dugout canoes, flat-bottomed bateaux and small sailing craft

Propulsion

Pole, paddle, or sail in wide reaches and coastal bays

St. Johns River

About 310 miles; flows north to the Atlantic at Mayport (SJRWMD)

Gulf rivers

Hillsborough to Tampa Bay; Peace to Charlotte Harbor

Steamboats

A later chapter (see FLH-0170); no start date asserted for the St. Johns

Apalachicola cotton

About 140,000 bales passed through the port in the winter of 1853 (local museum)

Cedar Key

First pencil mills opened in 1865 (Florida State Parks)

Cuba cattle

Driven overland to Punta Rassa

Spring-fed rivers

Ichetucknee, Silver, Rainbow, Weeki Wachee

Did You Know?

  • The St. Johns River flows north, one of the few U.S. rivers that does.
  • Glass-bottom boats were carrying tourists on the Silver River by the late 1870s.
  • Cattle bound for Cuba walked to Punta Rassa rather than floating down a river.

Vocabulary

Bateau: a flat-bottomed river boat with squared ends.

Dugout: a boat made by hollowing out a single log.

Landing: a cleared bank where boats could load, unload and tie up.
