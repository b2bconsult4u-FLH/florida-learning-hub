Pedro Menéndez de Avilés Dies in Spain

The founder of St. Augustine and first governor of Spanish Florida dies nine years after establishing Spain’s lasting foothold in Florida

By Wm. E. McMullen II

September 17, 1574 — Pedro Menéndez de Avilés—the Spanish admiral who founded St. Augustine and became the first governor of Spanish Florida—died in Santander, Spain. He was 55 years old.

Menéndez was in Santander preparing a royal fleet for King Philip II when illness spread among the assembled forces. His death came only nine years after he established the settlement that secured Spain’s lasting foothold in Florida.

Spain’s Mission in Florida

In 1565, Menéndez sailed from Spain with approximately 1,000 sailors, soldiers, and settlers. The Spanish Crown ordered him to remove the French settlement at Fort Caroline, establish a permanent colony, and protect the route used by Spanish ships carrying goods and treasure through the Straits of Florida.

Menéndez established St. Augustine on September 8, 1565, near the Timucua village of Seloy. The settlement became Spain’s principal base in Florida and is now recognized as the oldest continuously occupied European-established city in the continental United States.

St. Augustine was not established on an empty coast. Indigenous peoples had lived throughout Florida for thousands of years and maintained their own communities, political alliances, trade networks, and territorial boundaries. Spanish survival depended partly upon relationships—sometimes cooperative and sometimes hostile—with the Timucua and other Native peoples.

Fort Caroline and Matanzas

Soon after establishing St. Augustine, Menéndez led his soldiers north through heavy rain and difficult terrain to attack Fort Caroline, near present-day Jacksonville. Spanish forces captured the French settlement and killed most of its defenders, although some women and children were spared.

A French fleet commanded by Jean Ribault had sailed south to attack St. Augustine but was scattered by a powerful storm. Many of the shipwrecked French survivors eventually surrendered to Menéndez south of St. Augustine.

Menéndez ordered the execution of most of the prisoners near an inlet that became known as Matanzas—the Spanish word for “slaughters.”

The destruction of Fort Caroline and the killings at Matanzas ended France’s immediate attempt to establish a permanent colony in Florida. They also became inseparable from Menéndez’s historical legacy. To Spain, he was a successful commander who removed a rival colony and protected Spanish territory. To the French, particularly French Protestants, the events became remembered as a massacre driven by imperial and religious conflict.

Building Spanish Florida

Menéndez attempted to extend Spanish authority far beyond St. Augustine. Spanish soldiers, missionaries, and representatives traveled along Florida’s Atlantic and Gulf coasts and into areas that are now parts of Georgia and South Carolina.

These efforts brought Menéndez and his representatives into contact with the Timucua, Ais, Tequesta, Calusa, and other Indigenous peoples. Agreements were negotiated, forts and mission outposts were attempted, and interpreters were placed in Native communities. Some relationships began peacefully but deteriorated as Spanish demands for food, labor, religious conversion, and political obedience increased.

Many of Menéndez’s settlements and garrisons struggled with hunger, disease, isolation, resistance, and shortages of supplies. Several were abandoned or destroyed. St. Augustine survived, but Spain’s larger colonial project remained fragile.

A Complicated Florida Legacy

Menéndez’s death removed the forceful organizer who had directed Spain’s Florida enterprise from its beginning. Spanish officials and members of his extended family continued administering the colony, but they inherited settlements that remained expensive, difficult to supply, and dependent upon uncertain relationships with Indigenous communities.

Spanish Florida nevertheless endured for nearly two centuries after his death. St. Augustine became its military, religious, and administrative center. Spanish forts, missions, maritime routes, and alliances would shape Florida’s history for generations.

Pedro Menéndez de Avilés therefore occupies a complicated place in Florida history. He founded the settlement that became St. Augustine and secured Spain’s long presence in the region. He was also a military conqueror whose campaigns brought warfare, executions, colonial expansion, and profound disruption to French settlers and Indigenous communities.

Remembering the full story does not require choosing between presenting him as a hero or condemning him as a villain. It requires recognizing what his actions accomplished, who suffered because of them, and how the Florida he helped create emerged from conflict among Native peoples and competing European empires.

Nine years after stepping ashore at St. Augustine, Menéndez died an ocean away in Spain. The colony he left behind survived him—and became one of the foundations of Florida’s recorded colonial history.
