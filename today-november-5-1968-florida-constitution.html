Floridians Approve a New Framework for Government

1968 — Voters ratify major constitutional revisions, changing Florida’s executive and legislative framework while leaving the judicial article for later reform.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

On November 5, 1968, Florida voters ratified major constitutional revisions that became the foundation of the state’s modern governmental framework. The new document replaced most of the Constitution of 1885, while carrying forward the older judicial article until a later reform. The vote changed the basic rules under which elected officials, state agencies and local governments operated. It was a decision about how Florida would govern itself as well as a response to how much the state had changed. [1], [2]

A constitution can appear remote from ordinary life because its language concerns offices, powers and procedures. Yet those rules shape decisions about schools, taxation, public services and individual rights. The November vote was therefore more than an exercise in legal editing. Floridians were approving an operating framework for the institutions that acted in their name.

An older document meets a different Florida

Florida’s 1885 constitution came from a state very different from the one that existed by the 1960s. Over the intervening decades, population growth, expanding cities, tourism and new transportation networks transformed public needs. Government had to deal with a wider range of activities and a larger population. Rules designed in one era could become awkward restraints or preserve arrangements that no longer matched the distribution of people.

Revision also occurred amid national struggles over representation and civil rights. Understanding that setting does not mean claiming one ballot instantly resolved every inequality. It means recognizing that governmental design was being reconsidered while Floridians debated who should exercise power, how it should be distributed and how public institutions should respond to a changing society. [2], [3]

A constitution differs from an ordinary statute. Legislatures pass statutes within the powers and limits a constitution establishes. Replacing constitutional provisions changes the structure within which later laws are made. That is why revision requires a process beyond a routine legislative vote and why voter ratification carried such significance.

How the proposal reached voters

The Florida Senate’s constitutional preface identifies three joint resolutions adopted during a special legislative session running from June 24 through July 3, 1968. Together, the proposals covered the broad constitutional revision, suffrage and elections, and local government. Voters ratified the revisions on November 5. The document thus emerged through drafting, legislative action and public approval rather than through one official writing a replacement alone. [1]

That sequence illustrates a practical tension in constitutional change. A proposal must be detailed enough to govern effectively, but voters need to understand its direction and consequences. Legislators, lawyers, civic organizations and other participants can influence the text, yet ratification asks the electorate to authorize the resulting framework.

Approval did not mean that every voter agreed with every provision. Large constitutional proposals combine many choices. People may support the overall reform while disagreeing with particular arrangements. Historians should distinguish passage of a document from unanimous public enthusiasm for all its contents.

Changing the executive branch

The revised framework restored the office of lieutenant governor, which had been absent under the 1885 constitution. It also permitted a governor to seek a consecutive term within constitutional limits. These changes affected continuity, succession and accountability in the executive branch. An election could now offer voters the possibility of retaining an administration rather than necessarily moving to a different governor after one term. [3], [4]

The lieutenant governor’s office addressed another question: who should be available within the executive structure when leadership changed or assistance was needed? The answer involved constitutional design, not simply adding a new title. Offices matter because their rules define how authority continues when a person leaves, becomes unable to serve or moves into another position.

The revision also directed executive reorganization. Government programs had accumulated over time, and arranging administrative responsibilities more systematically was one goal of modernization. Reorganization could clarify responsibility, but the existence of an organizational chart did not guarantee effective management. Implementation still required legislation, appointments, budgets and competent daily administration. [4]

The contemporary Senate handbook explained the scale of that project: executive functions were to be organized into no more than twenty-five departments, apart from those provided for in the constitution. This was a concrete instruction to reorganize administration, not simply a general expression of support for efficiency. Officials still had to decide which responsibilities belonged together and who would supervise them. [4]

Legislative work and local choices

Annual regular legislative sessions were another important change associated with the 1968 framework. More frequent scheduled lawmaking gave the legislature a recurring opportunity to address state business. The timing of sessions matters because public needs do not wait indefinitely. At the same time, meeting more often does not by itself settle disagreements over priorities or spending. It changes the opportunity to act. [3]

The revision’s separate attention to local government recognized that governing Florida was not only a Tallahassee activity. Counties and municipalities delivered services and made decisions affecting residents closely. Constitutional rules helped define the relationship between state authority and local action. That relationship remains a recurring feature of Florida civic life, although later amendments and laws have changed particular details.

These structural subjects can seem less dramatic than an election between famous candidates. Their effects may be broader. Rules about who can act, when an institution meets and which level of government has responsibility shape thousands of later decisions. Constitutional history explains the channels through which political choices become public action.

What November 5 did not change

The 1968 revision did not replace every part of the old constitution at once. Article V, governing the judiciary, was carried forward from the 1885 document as amended. A major revision of that article came in 1972. Keeping those dates separate prevents a common misunderstanding: that every feature associated with modern Florida government originated in the same election. [1]

The constitution available online today also contains later amendments. A present-day provision cannot automatically be treated as the wording voters approved in 1968. Historical reading requires checking amendment notes and, when necessary, consulting the original text. The document is both a historical foundation and a framework repeatedly altered by subsequent decisions.

Why it matters

November 5, 1968, shows Floridians changing government through an established public process. The vote connected rapid social change with institutional design. It did not guarantee wise policy or eliminate conflict, but it changed the rules under which conflict would be debated and decisions made.

The anniversary offers an entry point into practical civics: why constitutions differ from laws, why succession rules matter and why governmental structure affects daily services. Florida history is not only the story of who held office. It is also the story of how residents authorized offices, limited power and revised the machinery of public life when an older framework no longer seemed sufficient.

Classroom Connection

Reading Prompt

Find three changes to governmental structure and explain the problem each was intended to address.

Glossary

Ratification — formal approval; constitution — basic framework and highest state legal authority subject to the U.S. Constitution; statute — law enacted by a legislature; succession — transfer of an office’s authority.

Reading Check

1. What happened on November 5? 2. Which article was carried forward and substantially revised later?

Think and Discuss

When should a society revise its basic rules rather than pass another ordinary law?

Short Writing Prompt

Explain why annual sessions could matter even if legislators continued disagreeing.

Teacher Notes and Suggested Answers

1. Voters ratified the constitutional revisions. 2. Article V, concerning the judiciary; major revision followed in 1972. Distinguish the original 1968 text from the constitution as amended today. This article is historical explanation, not legal advice about a current dispute.
