Gamble Plantation

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

How a Confederate Cabinet Officer Vanished Through the Florida Wilderness and Surfaced in England

A fugitive cabinet officer moved through Manatee and Sarasota with the help of local residents.

By the spring of 1865 the Confederacy that Judah P. Benjamin had served as Attorney General, Secretary of War and Secretary of State was finished. Benjamin had been one of its most visible cabinet officials and feared arrest and trial for treason. After traveling south with Jefferson Davis’s party, he separated from it in early May and turned toward Florida. What followed was a weeks-long disappearing act through the Florida wilderness that ended with Benjamin rebuilding a legal career in London.

A Fugitive Heading South

Benjamin traveled under assumed identities, at one stage as a Frenchman and, after crossing into Florida, as a farmer scouting land to buy, according to Michael Schene’s 1982 article in Tampa Bay History. Accounts place him in Brooksville, probably in late May or early June, before he was taken south toward the Manatee River. Who guided him is disputed: one family tradition credits Major John T. Lesley and another credits James McKay.

Refuge at the Gamble Mansion

The mansion was built in the late 1840s, with sources giving dates from about 1844 to 1850, by Major Robert Gamble, who established a sugar plantation of roughly 3,400 to 3,500 acres on the Manatee River. It was built largely by enslaved laborers and artisans, and parts of it, including the colonnade, are tabby, a mix of burned oyster-shell lime, sand and water. Florida State Parks calls it the only surviving plantation house in South Florida. Gamble fell into debt and sold the property, with sources giving 1856, 1857 or 1859. By the war the occupant was Captain Archibald McNeill, described in a 2011 National Register nomination as a blockade-runner and later a Confederate commissary agent.

Benjamin reportedly passed as a Mr. Howard, though another account gives the alias Boyd. Florida State Parks words the claim cautiously, saying he is thought to have sheltered there. Schene writes that Federal forces searched the mansion repeatedly and nearly captured him during one surprise visit, though versions differ on the details.

Hidden Among the Beef Wagons

When the danger grew, Benjamin was moved across the river to the home of Captain Frederick Tresca, described as experienced in evading the blockade. Sources differ on how long he stayed, from about two weeks to a month. Hiram McLeod joined Tresca in the plan to get him out. According to the Sarasota County Historical Commission’s marker, Benjamin was concealed under a load of beef in a wagon and carried to the Sarasota home of William Whitaker, a settler on Sarasota Bay since the early 1840s.

A Small Boat and the Open Gulf

From Whitaker Bayou, Tresca and McLeod sailed Benjamin into Sarasota Bay, bound for the Bahamas. The marker gives the date as June 23, 1865, though Schene gives May 23, and this discrepancy is unresolved. The boat is described as an open sloop in the marker and as a yawl in Schene. By one historian’s account a Union search party boarded the boat, and in several accounts Benjamin posed as the cook. The party reached Bimini on or around July 10, 1865. From there the route is harder to follow: one account says a boat carrying Benjamin toward Nassau broke apart and sank and that he was rescued, and he probably reached Nassau around July 21. He went on to Havana and reached Southampton, England, on August 30, 1865.

A Story Historians Still Untangle

Historians do not agree on every detail. Some popular accounts have Benjamin sailing from the Manatee River near the mansion, while the marker and Schene place the departure at Whitaker Bayou. Family traditions of the Tresca, McLeod, Whitaker, Lesley and McKay families preserve different versions. The sources agree on the outline: a fugitive Confederate cabinet officer moved through the region with local help, left Florida by small boat for the Bahamas and eventually reached England.

What Benjamin Found in England

In England, Benjamin studied English law and rose through the British bar. He died in Paris on May 6, 1884. The mansion he sheltered in was also the center of a slave-labor sugar economy, and visitors to Gamble Plantation Historic State Park today encounter both histories.

Quick Facts

Fugitive

Judah P. Benjamin, Confederate Secretary of State

Refuge

Gamble Mansion, Ellenton, Manatee County; occupied by Captain Archibald McNeill

Mansion

Built in the late 1840s by Major Robert Gamble; tabby colonnade

Final Florida helpers

Captain Frederick Tresca and Hiram McLeod

Last Florida stop

William Whitaker’s home on Sarasota Bay

Departure

Whitaker Bayou; June 23, 1865 per the marker (Schene says May 23)

First landfall

Bimini, on or about July 10, 1865

Arrived in England

Southampton, August 30, 1865

Death

Paris, May 6, 1884

Today

Judah P. Benjamin Confederate Memorial at Gamble Plantation Historic State Park

Did You Know?

  • The Gamble Mansion is described as the only surviving plantation house in South Florida.
  • The mansion’s tabby is made from burned oyster shells, sand and water.
  • One account has Benjamin hiding under a wagonload of beef.

Vocabulary

Tabby: a coastal building material made of burned oyster-shell lime, sand and water.

Alias: a false name used to hide one’s identity.

Sloop: a small single-masted sailing boat.
