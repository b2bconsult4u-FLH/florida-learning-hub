The Treasure Hunters of the Treasure Coast

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

How a Three-Hundred-Year-Old Disaster Became a Living, Working Florida Industry

The hunt for the 1715 treasure never actually stopped.

In the summer of 2025, three hundred and ten years after a hurricane destroyed a Spanish treasure fleet off Florida’s east coast (FLH-0125), a salvage crew led by Captain Levin Shavers recovered more than a thousand silver and gold coins from a 1715 wreck site off the Treasure Coast. The find was announced in a September 30, 2025 press release by 1715 Fleet – Queens Jewels, LLC, the private company that holds the salvage rights to the 1715 wrecks. The company valued the recovery at about $1 million, which is its own estimate, not an independent appraisal.

From Beachcomber to Industry Founder

Kip Wagner, a building contractor who beachcombed along Florida’s east coast, began finding old Spanish coins on the beach after storms. He turned that curiosity into historical research and, with partners, formed the Real Eight Company, reported to have been incorporated in 1961. It was among the first organized salvage operations to work the 1715 sites. Mel Fisher, then running a dive shop in California, heard of the Real Eight finds in 1962 and moved to Florida with a team of divers in 1963, according to the 1715 Fleet Society, which is affiliated with the current rights holder.

The Search for the Atocha

Having worked the 1715 sites, Fisher turned to the Nuestra Señora de Atocha, the 1622 wreck described in FLH-0129. He and his company, Treasure Salvors, began the search in 1969, and it consumed sixteen years. In 1975, according to the Mel Fisher Maritime Museum, his son Dirk found five bronze cannon from the wreck. Days later Dirk, his wife Angel and diver Rick Gage were killed when their salvage boat capsized and sank. The exact date and cause of the accident have not been confirmed from a contemporary record. The family continued the search.

A Fight With the State

The Atocha lay about forty nautical miles west of Key West, in a location the courts later found to be on the Outer Continental Shelf, outside state-owned submerged land. According to the U.S. Supreme Court’s opinion, after the wreck was located in 1971 Treasure Salvors signed a one-year contract with Florida, with similar contracts following. The company was to receive 75 percent of the appraised value of recovered material and the State 25 percent.

The dispute turned on who owned what had been recovered. Florida officials seized artifacts, and the company sued in federal court. The case reached the Supreme Court as Florida Department of State v. Treasure Salvors, Inc., 458 U.S. 670, decided July 1, 1982. In a plurality opinion the Court held that the Eleventh Amendment did not bar the federal court’s order directing state officials to turn over the artifacts, but also that the court could not decide the State’s own ownership claim without its consent. It was a mixed result on state immunity and federal admiralty process, not a simple ruling that salvors own whatever they recover outside state waters.

Today’s the Day

On July 20, 1985, Kane Fisher, captain of the salvage vessel Dauntless, reported that the crew had found the main body of the wreck. The museum quotes his message as “Put away the charts; we’ve found the main pile!” Mel Fisher’s company has put the value of the recovery at about $450 million, a company estimate and not an appraisal.

How the Industry Works Today

As reported by Jacksonville Today in October 2025, Florida law treats abandoned shipwreck artifacts on state lands and in state waters as state property, but the state can issue permits for recovery, and disturbing such sites without permission can bring felony charges. The same report says up to roughly 20 percent of recovered material may go to the state, a share this article has not confirmed in the statute. For the 1715 wrecks, CBS News (2015) and Jacksonville Today (2025) report that a federal court gave Queens Jewels exclusive salvage rights, with the court overseeing its finds. Some archaeologists question whether for-profit recovery serves shared cultural heritage as well as state-led or academic work, while others point to the private capital and risk that make recoveries possible.

A Story Still Being Written

In 2015, the three-hundredth anniversary of the disaster, the Queens Jewels crew reported recovering 350 gold coins, including nine “Royals” struck as presentation pieces for King Philip V, which the company valued at about $4.5 million (CBS News).

Did You Know?

* A 1982 U.S. Supreme Court case over Atocha artifacts produced a mixed ruling, not a simple win for salvors.

* Salvage companies’ dollar values are their own estimates, not appraisals.

* The 1715 wrecks are worked today under the supervision of a federal court.

Vocabulary

Plurality opinion: a court opinion supported by the most justices but fewer than a majority.

Admiralty law: the body of law governing maritime matters such as salvage.

Outer Continental Shelf: the seabed beyond a state’s submerged lands, under federal jurisdiction.
