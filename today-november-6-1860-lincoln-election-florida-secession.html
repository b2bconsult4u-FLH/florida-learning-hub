An Election Pushes Florida Toward Secession

1860 — Lincoln’s election prompts Florida leaders toward secession. The article follows the decisions between Election Day and January 1861 and explains slavery’s central role.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

On November 6, 1860, Abraham Lincoln won the United States presidential election. In Florida, his victory intensified efforts by political leaders to leave the Union. Governor Madison Starke Perry urged preparation for secession, and the legislature later authorized a convention in Tallahassee. Florida did not secede on Election Day: its convention adopted an ordinance of secession on January 10, 1861. The November election was a turning point in the chain of decisions that carried the state toward civil war. [1], [2], [3]

The distinction between a cause, a political response and a formal act is essential. An election did not automatically remove Florida from the United States. People holding power made choices in the weeks that followed. Those choices were shaped above all by the determination of secessionists to protect slavery and the social order built around it. [3], [4]

A national election divided by slavery

The 1860 contest involved four major candidates: Republican Abraham Lincoln, Northern Democrat Stephen Douglas, Southern Democrat John C. Breckinridge and Constitutional Union candidate John Bell. Their competing coalitions reflected divisions over slavery, its expansion and the future of the Union. Lincoln won through the Electoral College without obtaining a majority of all popular votes cast nationally. [1]

This electoral geography mattered. Lincoln could win the presidency through support concentrated outside the slaveholding South. For Southern leaders accustomed to defending slavery through national political influence, the result demonstrated that a president could be elected without their support. Their fear concerned future power as well as the incoming administration’s immediate intentions.

Lincoln’s opposition to the expansion of slavery did not mean that emancipation everywhere began with his election. The Emancipation Proclamation belonged to the later wartime sequence, and the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery nationally in 1865. Keeping those developments separate avoids turning the election into something it was not. The immediate issue was a change in national political leadership and the response it provoked.

There was also a gap between election and inauguration. Lincoln was president-elect while Florida’s secession movement organized; he did not take office until March 1861. The crisis therefore developed during a transfer of national leadership. Floridians deciding on separation were reacting partly to what they expected an incoming administration to do, not only to actions it had already taken. [5]

Whose Florida was represented?

Florida’s political institutions did not speak through an electorate including all adults. Enslaved people had no vote in the decisions governing their lives. Women were also excluded from voting. White male voters and officeholders operated within a society where ownership of enslaved people carried economic and political influence. Calling secession the unqualified wish of “all Floridians” would erase those exclusions.

The people with the least political voice had the most fundamental interests at stake. For an enslaved person, the struggle over slavery concerned family, labor, bodily autonomy and freedom. For an enslaver, it concerned maintaining power over other human beings and preserving property claims recognized by existing law. Those interests were not equivalent, even though both were affected by the same election.

Florida Memory’s Civil War guide identifies fear for slavery and the established Southern order as central to the secessionist response. That explanation is more specific than an abstract reference to “states’ rights.” The historical question is which rights leaders sought to defend and whose rights their system denied. [4]

From election results to a convention

Governor Perry encouraged Florida to prepare for separation and cooperation with other Southern states. On November 30, he signed legislation calling a convention to consider secession. The convention assembled in Tallahassee in early January. Its delegates debated a decision that would alter Florida’s relationship with the national government and expose the state to enormous uncertainty. [2], [3]

The sequence reveals how institutions can accelerate a crisis. A governor’s call, legislative authorization, delegate selection and convention proceedings converted political alarm into formal action. Each stage involved people who could argue, organize and vote. Secession did not happen because a calendar advanced from November to January; it happened through decisions made within an unequal political system.

The convention’s January 10 vote was sixty-two to seven in favor of the ordinance. That result documents the delegates’ decision, not the private views of every resident. Even among people admitted to political participation, unanimity should not be assumed. Later wartime dissent would further demonstrate that Florida’s population did not act as one undivided body. [2], [3], [4]

What the ordinance claimed

Florida’s ordinance declared that its political connection with the United States was dissolved and that the state was a sovereign, independent nation. The language asserted a transformation in authority. It did not make the national government accept that claim. The dispute over whether a state could leave the Union became inseparable from the conflict that followed. [2]

Reading the document closely helps distinguish legal language from explanation. The ordinance states the action delegates purported to take. Understanding why they took it requires additional evidence: speeches, messages, correspondence and the social structure they sought to preserve. A short formal document cannot carry the entire historical interpretation by itself.

The same principle applies to the election. Vote totals establish a result, but they do not alone explain how Floridians understood that result. Researchers need different kinds of sources to connect national politics with local decisions. Chronology provides the skeleton; evidence about motives and institutions gives it meaning.

Consequences beyond the convention chamber

Florida became one of the states that formed the Confederacy. War brought coastal blockade, military occupation, shortages, divided loyalties and losses that reached far beyond those who had voted for secession. Ports, rivers, farms and salt-making operations became parts of the military struggle. The state’s geography shaped its wartime role, but that role followed political decisions made before major battles occurred. [4]

For enslaved Floridians, the war also created opportunities to seek freedom, escape Confederate control and support Union forces. Emancipation did not arrive simply as a gift from a distant event; people acted within dangerous circumstances as the conflict changed what was possible. Their experiences belong alongside the decisions of governors and convention delegates.

Why it matters

November 6, 1860, connects Florida to a national election whose consequences reached into nearly every part of the state. The date helps readers see how a national result became a local constitutional crisis through deliberate action. It also requires clarity about slavery’s central role and about the many Floridians excluded from making the decision.

The anniversary is an opportunity to study how political leaders respond when power changes hands. Florida’s secession was neither an automatic consequence nor a harmless declaration. It was a consequential choice made in defense of an unequal order, followed by a war that transformed the state and the nation.

Classroom Connection

Reading Prompt

Build a timeline separating the election, the call for a convention and the secession vote.

Glossary

Secession — claimed withdrawal from a political union; electorate — people entitled to vote; ordinance — formal enactment by a governing body; emancipation — release from slavery.

Reading Check

1. Did Florida secede on November 6? 2. What institution did secessionist leaders primarily seek to protect?

Think and Discuss

Why must historians ask who was excluded when describing a political decision as the will of a state?

Short Writing Prompt

Explain the difference between an event that contributes to a decision and the decision itself.

Teacher Notes and Suggested Answers

1. No; the convention adopted the ordinance January 10, 1861. 2. Slavery and the associated economic and racial order. Keep the election, wartime emancipation and the Thirteenth Amendment on separate dates.
