Bellamy Road

The Wagon Road That Broke Axles and Opened Florida's Interior

Florida Learning Hub · Mini Article · Florida Frontier Defense & Military History

In 1826, Florida had a brand-new federal road, and travelers hated it. Stumps were supposed to be cut close to the ground so a wagon's axle could clear them, but plenty were left standing tall enough to catch and snap an axle anyway. The road earned a nickname that stuck for a century: the “stump knocker.” Rough as it was, this crude sand track — the first wagon road built to connect Florida's Panhandle to its Atlantic coast — opened the territory's interior to overland travel.

This article opens Fort Pillar V's look at Military Roads & Transportation: the network of routes, river crossings, and supply lines that let soldiers, mail, and settlers move across Florida's difficult terrain.

Travelers complained that some of the stumps left standing in the roadbed were tall enough to snap a wagon's axle.

A Road the Army Had to Build

Before 1821, Pensacola and St. Augustine — Florida's two major cities — were separated by nearly 400 miles of wilderness with no practical overland connection. Narrow trails first used by Native Americans and later by Spanish missionaries crossed the interior, but travel was difficult.

After the United States acquired Florida in 1821, territorial residents petitioned Congress for a road. In 1824 Congress authorized $20,000 for a route from Pensacola to St. Augustine and specified river crossings along the way. Captain Daniel E. Burch oversaw the project under Quartermaster General Thomas S. Jesup. Army crews built the western stretch, while the eastern portion was contracted out.

Cutting the “Stump Knocker”

With Territorial Governor William Duval's endorsement, John Bellamy of Monticello won the contract to build the eastern section for $13,500, using equipment, enslaved laborers from his plantation, and Native American guides. He completed his stretch in 1826. Congress had called for a road 25 feet wide, but Bellamy's contract required only 16 feet. Stumps left in the roadbed jolted wagons and reportedly broke axles.

Bellamy's route largely followed the older Spanish Camino Real, the mission trail linking St. Augustine with the Apalachee province. The road crossed the Aucilla and Suwannee rivers by ferry before reaching the St. Johns River at Picolata.

Why This Mattered for Florida

The road made overland travel between Florida's principal territorial cities substantially easier and encouraged movement into the interior. That expansion also increased pressure on lands occupied by Native Floridians and contributed to tensions that preceded the Second Seminole War.

Tallahassee, near the midpoint between Pensacola and St. Augustine, was selected as the territorial capital in 1824. Sections of the historic road remain identifiable today, preserving part of the transportation system that helped reshape territorial Florida.

Vocabulary

  • Federal road — a road authorized or funded by the U.S. Congress.
  • Quartermaster — a military officer responsible for supplies and transportation.
  • Natural bridge — a place where a river passes underground, leaving solid ground above that travelers can cross.
