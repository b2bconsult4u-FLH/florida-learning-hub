Today in Florida History · October 5

Madison Starke Perry Takes Office as Florida Approaches a Crossroads

October 5, 1857 — Florida's fourth governor began a term that combined railroad development and a boundary settlement with preparations for secession.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

A Governor for a Young State

On October 5, 1857, Madison Starke Perry began his term as Florida's fourth governor. The Museum of Florida History dates his administration from that day to October 7, 1861. Those four years carried Florida from development projects and an unsettled state boundary into secession and civil war.

Perry was born in South Carolina in 1814 and came to Florida during the 1830s. He became a prominent plantation owner. That background matters: his political world was closely connected to the plantation economy and the institution of slavery.

Railways and a Border

The state museum credits Perry with encouraging railway construction and helping settle a long-running boundary dispute with Georgia. Both concerns show how much work remained in building the young state. Transportation connected settlements and markets, while a recognized boundary clarified where one state's authority ended and another's began.

These subjects can look routine beside the coming war. Yet they remind us that Florida's nineteenth-century governors dealt with practical questions of movement, land and administration as well as national political conflict.

Preparing for Secession

Perry also anticipated the possibility that Florida would leave the United States. In 1858 he urged the revival of the state militia. After Abraham Lincoln's election in November 1860, he pressed for preparations for secession and cooperation with other Southern states.

Florida Memory identifies fears about the future of slavery as central to the secessionists' position. On January 10, 1861, the convention adopted Florida's ordinance of secession; members signed it the following day. Separating those dates avoids confusing the vote with the signing.

Why It Matters

The October 5 inauguration was not itself an act of secession. It began an administration whose choices helped move Florida toward that break. Reading Perry's term as a whole connects the state's development ambitions with the political and economic system its leaders sought to preserve.

An inauguration date can therefore open a much larger story. Florida's railways, boundaries, plantations and militia were parts of the same state entering an exceptionally consequential four years.
