The SS Tarpon Sinks in the Gulf

September 1, 1937 — A longtime lifeline among Florida's Panhandle ports is lost off Panama City

Before dawn on September 1, 1937, the steamship SS Tarpon encountered rough seas and began taking on water while traveling from Pensacola toward Panama City. Heavily loaded with more than 200 tons of cargo, the vessel had less than five inches of freeboard—the distance between the waterline and the deck—as it entered the Gulf of Mexico.

The Tarpon had been one of the Florida Panhandle's most dependable transportation links for more than 30 years. Beginning in 1903, it made weekly runs among Mobile, Pensacola, Panama City, Apalachicola, and Carrabelle. Long before modern highways connected these coastal communities, the ship carried passengers, mail, building materials, food, fuel, and other essential supplies.

A Desperate Fight to Save the Ship

As the September gale intensified, the crew tried to save the vessel by pumping water and throwing cargo overboard. First mate L. E. Danford turned toward shore, hoping to beach the ship, but Captain Willis Green Barrow ordered it back on course. The Tarpon soon sank stern-first approximately eight nautical miles off Panama City.

The vessel carried no radio, so no distress call reached a Coast Guard cutter operating only several miles away. In an era before dependable ship-to-shore communication, the people aboard were left to survive the open Gulf with little chance of immediate rescue.

Thirteen Survivors

Thirteen of the 31 people aboard survived. Eighteen—including Captain Barrow—are believed to have died. Crewman Adley Baker reached shore near Philips Inlet after approximately 25 hours in the water and reported the disaster. Some other survivors remained afloat for as long as 33 hours before being rescued.

A federal investigation blamed Barrow's decision to continue on course despite the vessel's dangerous condition. The investigating board also recommended requiring radios aboard vessels traveling beyond sight of land. The tragedy showed how quickly an overloaded ship, severe weather, and the absence of modern communications could turn a routine coastal voyage into a disaster.

A Protected Florida Shipwreck

Today, the wreck lies in about 95 feet of water and is protected as a Florida Underwater Archaeological Preserve. Divers can visit the remains as an underwater historical site, but the wreck and its artifacts are protected by law. The Tarpon was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2001.

The shipwreck preserves more than the story of one tragic voyage. It recalls a time when coastal steamships connected Florida communities that roads had not yet reached—and when the Gulf served as both a highway and a hazard for the people who depended upon it.
