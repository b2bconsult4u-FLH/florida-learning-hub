Settlement Patterns

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

Why Pioneer Families Stopped Where They Did — and How Those Decisions Built the Florida We Know Today

The settlement frontier did not move steadily south. It advanced unevenly, onto land where others already lived.

Where American-era settlers put their homesteads, and where later towns and county seats appeared, was shaped by geography, economics, family ties and the decisions of governments and the military. It was also shaped by who was already there. Florida’s interior was home to Seminole communities and earlier Native peoples long before the territory passed to the United States in 1821, and the Spanish had ranched and settled parts of the peninsula for centuries. The American pioneer wave of the 1820s through the 1860s moved onto land that others had used and, in many cases, claimed.

What Settlers Looked For

A family choosing a site weighed a handful of practical matters. High ground mattered because Florida’s low flatwoods flood in the rainy season. Farmers reportedly read soil from vegetation, taking hardwood hammocks as a sign of richer ground than longleaf pine on pale sand. Water from springs, creeks or wells was basic, and timber stood close at hand for building. No single source ranks these concerns.

North First

Settlement began in the north and moved south in stages. The State of Florida’s historical overview notes that settlers in the territorial period came largely from Virginia, the Carolinas and Georgia, and that plantations were concentrated in Middle Florida around Tallahassee, made the capital in 1824. Florida Memory adds that by 1840 the region produced about 80 percent of the territory’s cotton and that by 1830 its enslaved population exceeded its white population. Its plantation agriculture rested on the labor of enslaved people, who were not settlers by choice.

Another early area lay around the Alachua Prairie, which had been a stronghold of the Alachua band of the Seminole under Ahaya, called Cowkeeper, by the mid-1700s. Newnansville, a trail junction, served as the Alachua County seat from 1828 to 1854.

Roads, War and Land Grants

The Pensacola–St. Augustine road, completed in 1826, was built mostly by enslaved laborers, according to a state marker. The Second Seminole War (1835 to 1842) was a federal campaign to remove the Seminole people to the West; the National Park Service notes that thousands were captured and sent west and that a few hundred remained. The Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe remain in Florida today.

In 1842 the Armed Occupation Act offered 160 acres to a settler who built a house, cultivated five acres and lived on the land five years, on unsettled land south of a line near Palatka and not within two miles of a garrisoned post. Reported permit totals differ, from about 950 to nearly 1,200 claimants.

Forts Into Towns

Some towns grew where military posts had stood, though the sequence varied. Fort Brooke was established at the mouth of the Hillsborough River in 1824, and the village of Tampa developed just north of it. Fort King was built in 1827; Ocala was platted beside it in 1846, planned rather than grown from the fort. Fort Meade traces its origin to a post established in 1849. At Fort Myers the Army built a post in 1850 and abandoned it in 1858, and a civilian community took its name later.

County Seats and the Map

County seats came from a mix of trail junctions, forts, railroad plans and land donations. Ocala was chosen in 1846. Gainesville was founded in 1854 on the planned Florida Railroad route and took the county seat from Newnansville, so the railroad, not an existing center, set the location. Brooksville has been Hernando County’s seat since 1856. Bartow became Polk County’s seat after its first courthouse was built in 1867. The towns, roads and county lines of the interior still carry traces of these decisions, and of the people who were there first.

Did You Know?

  • Ocala was planned in 1846 beside Fort King rather than growing from the fort gate.
  • Gainesville was laid out for a railroad that had not yet arrived.
  • By 1830 Middle Florida’s enslaved population exceeded its white population.

Vocabulary

Homestead: a home and the land around it, claimed and worked by a family.

County seat: the town where a county’s government is based.

Plat: to lay out a town or lots on a map.
