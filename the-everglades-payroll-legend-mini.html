The Everglades Payroll Legend

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

The Search for Evidence Behind Florida’s Buried Payroll Legend

Cultural value is different from historical evidence.

A single sentence, in versions that vary slightly from one telling to the next, is the core of one of Florida’s buried-treasure legends: that a Confederate paymaster, chased by the enemy, buried the army’s payroll at a junction of two creeks where the land rises like a camel’s back. Treasure books, websites and forum posts present it as the words behind a story of roughly $200,000 in gold coins buried in the Everglades near the end of the Civil War. This article does not claim the sentence comes from any letter, diary or report. In the retellings examined, no document, writer or archive is named for it.

What the Legend Claims

In its commonly repeated form, a Confederate paymaster retreating as Union forces closed in chose to bury the unit’s payroll rather than risk capture. The total is usually given as $200,000 in gold, sometimes with paper currency that raises it toward a million dollars. The site is a point where two creeks meet, on ground that rises like a camel’s back, with the money in the western of two humps. One variant adds a blockade-running captain called John Riley and a separate burial in Broward County; no traceable source for him or his ship was found, so it is best treated as an unattributed legend. For documented blockade running, see FLH-0102.

Looking for the Source

A claim is only as strong as its evidence, and this one has very little that can be traced. None of the retellings examined says where the quoted account exists. No paymaster, regiment or unit is named, and the only date is near the end of the war. The chain of retellings could not be followed back to a first appearance, though archives and newspapers were not searched in full, so a traceable origin may exist.

Cotton Wood (FLH-0106) offers a contrast. C. Wickliffe Yulee published an account in the Florida Historical Quarterly in 1909 of events at the family’s plantation near Archer in May 1865. By his account, he buried boxes he was told held the private papers and effects of Confederate President Jefferson Davis, and a Union officer’s report quoted in the account describes a trunk, a chest and personal effects, not treasure. Accounts differ on what was buried and recovered. Cotton Wood is not evidence of a buried Confederate fortune, but it offers a named place, a named participant and a published account a reader can check. The payroll legend offers a quotation with no such trail.

A Story That Keeps Changing Shape

The story shifts with the teller. Some versions put the burial near Hendry County and others elsewhere in South Florida, and the amounts vary from $200,000 in gold to nearly a million with paper currency. Many treasure stories share a structure: a Confederate party pursued by Union forces buries treasure at a vividly described landmark. The sources examined support no explanation with a document, though a reused narrative template is a plausible reading of a pattern with no traceable origin.

The Knights of the Golden Circle

Some retellings link the legend to the Knights of the Golden Circle, a secret society active before and during the Civil War and associated with expansionist, pro-slavery politics, around which a body of treasure folklore has grown. No scholarly assessment of those claims was located here, so no historians’ consensus is asserted. For the Everglades legend the link supplies no document; a famous society adds color to a story, not evidence.

Decades of Searching, Nothing Found

No documented, verified recovery tied to this legend was found. Online recollections describe treasure hunters searching South Florida with metal detectors as early as the 1960s, but these are informal and unverified. That does not prove the legend false, since failing to find an object is not the same as its absence. But with no traceable source, a shifting location, varying amounts and a shape shared with other tales, the legend appears to rest on its own repetition.

The areas associated with the legend lie in or near Everglades National Park and Big Cypress National Preserve, where federal regulations prohibit disturbing cultural and archeological resources (36 CFR 2.1). The Seminole and Miccosukee Tribes have reservation lands in this region and live and work in South Florida today; entry to tribal lands is governed by the tribes.

No document, writer or archive identified in the sources examined

Not named in any version located

Did You Know?

* The legend’s key quotation has no identified document, writer or archive behind it.

* A Union officer’s report in the Cotton Wood account describes a trunk, a chest and personal effects, not treasure.

* Digging for treasure on National Park Service land is prohibited.

Vocabulary

Primary source: a record created at the time of an event or by a participant.

Folklore: stories passed along by tradition, not backed by documents.

Provenance: the documented history of where a record or object came from.
