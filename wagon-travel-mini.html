Wagon Travel

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

The Grueling, Glorious, and Irreversible Journey That Brought Pioneer Families to Florida

They were betting their lives on the road ahead.

The decision to go was the hardest part. A family in south Georgia or the Carolinas that chose to move to Florida in the pioneer era was selling the farm, leaving neighbors and family graves, and loading everything that mattered onto a wagon or two. It was not the Great Plains migration of popular imagination. It was a move south through country that grew less familiar and roads that grew worse. Few firsthand accounts of the Georgia-to-Florida journey survive, and this article says plainly where the record runs out.

The Wagon and What It Carried

The wagon was almost certainly not a Conestoga. The National Park Service describes the Conestoga as a large wagon built mainly to haul freight, not to carry emigrants. A family moving south more likely used an ordinary four-wheeled farm wagon, built by a local wainwright or by the farmer himself, with a canvas cover if the family had one. No dimensions for Florida-bound wagons have been established.

Every item had to justify its weight. Cast-iron cookware, the broadax, the auger and seed corn and beans are the kind of goods a homestead could not do without, though the Full’s detailed packing list is reasoned from the needs of settlement, not taken from a documented inventory.

Mules, Oxen and Cattle

Oxen and mules both pulled emigrant wagons. On the western trails the National Park Service reports that oxen were used more often than mules, but no comparable count exists for Georgia-to-Florida travel, so the mix on this road is not established. The draft animals were the first tools of the homestead, and many families also drove a few cattle alongside the wagon. One later recollection, by Eliza Allen, describes ox-drawn covered wagons on a move from Decatur County, Georgia, to Manatee County, Florida, in about 1874: the party left in the fall and arrived in December, after roughly six weeks. It dates from the 1870s, not the 1840s, and survives only in a volunteer-run genealogy transcription.

Sand and Mud

Florida’s sandy soils and heavy summer rains posed two opposite problems for wheeled travel. Deep sand made a loaded wagon hard to pull, and wet weather could leave roads soft and rutted. These descriptions are general; no period account of a specific stretch of road has been located for this article.

River Crossings

Rivers were among the most concentrated risks of the journey. Families entering from Georgia commonly met the Suwannee, and those continuing toward the Gulf coast could meet rivers such as the Withlacoochee or the Peace. Some streams could be forded; others required ferries, and a river in flood could force a wait. Ferries were a matter of public regulation. The State Archives of Florida holds an act of the Territorial Legislative Council, dated February 15, 1834, authorizing William Brinton Hooker to establish a ferry across the Suwannee, and a petition of about 1842 asking that Samuel Russell be allowed to establish another. Florida State Parks notes that Hooker built a ferry on the Suwannee in the mid-1830s. The items examined give no ferry rates, so the cost of a crossing is not stated.

Camp and Arrival

Each day’s travel ended with work: caring for the animals, gathering firewood, cooking over an open fire and arranging a place to sleep, under or inside the wagon if the family had one. The specifics varied by family and are not documented here. There was rarely a dramatic arrival. The wagon rolled forward until the driver chose to stop, and the stopping place became the beginning of the homestead. The wagon might serve as first shelter and then as the farm wagon, though no source is cited for that pattern.

Quick Facts

Wagon type

Most likely an ordinary farm wagon; the Conestoga was mainly a freight wagon (NPS)

Draft animals

Oxen and mules both used; on western trails oxen more often (NPS); Florida mix not established

Daily distance

10 to 15 miles a day on the western trails (NPS); no Florida figure located

Travel season

Not established for the 1840s; one 1870s recollection left in the fall and arrived in December

Time on the road

About six weeks in one 1870s recollection (Decatur County, Georgia, to Manatee County)

Rivers

Forded where possible; ferried where authorized; waited out in high water

Documented Suwannee ferries

Act of February 15, 1834 for William B. Hooker; petition about 1842 for Samuel Russell

Records

Diaries, letters and descendants’ oral histories, where they survive

Did You Know?

  • The Conestoga wagon was mainly a freight wagon, not the usual choice for emigrant families.
  • The Territorial Legislative Council authorized Suwannee River ferries by act and petition in the 1830s and 1840s.
  • Genealogists often find the journey as a blank between a Georgia census and a Florida one.

Vocabulary

Wainwright: a craftsman who builds and repairs wagons.

Ford: a shallow place where a river can be crossed on foot or by wagon.

Draft animal: an animal used to pull heavy loads.
