Frontier Bridges

“One of the Best and Strongest Field Fortifications”

Florida Learning Hub · Mini Article · Florida Frontier Defense & Military History

On January 1, 1837, Lieutenant Colonel William S. Foster wrote a letter to General R. Jones describing what his men had just finished building on the banks of the Hillsborough River. “That Fort & Bridge,” he wrote, “form one of the best and strongest field fortifications ever erected against Indians.” He had arrived on this spot only a month earlier with 430 men, tasked with rebuilding a bridge and fort that Seminole forces had already destroyed once.

This article closes Fort Pillar V's exploration of Florida's military roads and transportation, turning to the fixed river crossings — the bridges, not ferries or fords — that the Army built, defended, and repeatedly rebuilt across Florida's contested supply routes.

“That Fort & Bridge form one of the best and strongest field fortifications ever erected against Indians.”

A Bridge Worth an Entire Fort

The Fort King Road, the essential supply artery connecting Fort Brooke to Fort King, crossed the Hillsborough River at a single unavoidable point. The crossing was so strategically important that the Army built an entire fort, originally called Fort Alabama, specifically to protect the bridge.

The first bridge and fort did not survive the war's early months. Seminole forces destroyed both, and on December 1, 1836, Foster arrived to rebuild them. His men completed two blockhouses, a large storehouse, and the fort by December 19 — just eighteen days. Foster then departed with 180 men and twenty-five wagons of provisions and forage to resupply Fort Armstrong, leaving the remainder of his command to finish the bridge and a powder magazine.

A Target the Enemy Kept Returning To

Fort Foster's strategic importance made its bridge a recurring target. Historical research describes repeated Seminole attempts to burn the Fort King Road bridge, recognizing that destroying the crossing could sever the Army's supply line to Fort King and posts beyond it.

Fort Foster's role extended beyond its own bridge. Six other forts — Fort Dade, Fort Cooper, Fort Drane, Fort Clinch, Fort Izard, and Fort King — were constructed and resupplied through Fort Foster, making this crossing an important chokepoint in the Second Seminole War-era fort network.

Why This Mattered for Florida

Frontier bridges were the fixed, defended links in the supply chain: roads, rivers, wagon trains, and ferries all depended on critical crossings remaining open. Foster's own words reflect the Army's hard-won understanding that a bridge worth defending could be worth an entire fort built around it.

With bridges, Fort Pillar V's transportation story comes full circle — from road to river to wagon to ferry to the fixed crossings that kept Florida's frontier fort system connected and supplied.

Vocabulary

  • Chokepoint — a narrow, unavoidable passage controlling access to a wider area.
  • Magazine — a secure storage building for gunpowder and ammunition.
  • Garrison — soldiers stationed at and responsible for defending a fort.

Discussion Questions

  • Why might a single bridge have been valuable enough to justify building an entire fort to protect it?
  • What does it mean that other forts depended on resupply through Fort Foster's bridge crossing?
