Today in Florida History · November 2

Election Day Violence Begins in Ocoee

1920 — Mose Norman’s attempt to vote leads into the Ocoee Massacre, the destruction of a Black community and the lasting consequences for property and voting rights.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

On November 2, 1920, an attempt by a Black resident to vote in Ocoee helped trigger a campaign of white violence that destroyed a community. Mose Norman was denied access to the polls. Armed white men later searched for him at the home of July Perry. A confrontation there, Perry’s subsequent lynching and mob attacks continuing into November 3 became known as the Ocoee Massacre. The violence drove Black residents from the Orange County town and stripped families of homes, property and the ability to live where they had built their lives. [1], [2]

The date must be handled carefully. November 2 was Election Day and the beginning of the crisis, not the date on which every killing, fire and forced departure occurred. The destruction unfolded across more than one day, and the consequences lasted for generations. Telling the story accurately requires following that sequence while recognizing the limits of the surviving evidence.

A right on paper and barriers at the polls

The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited denying voting rights on account of race. In practice, Black Floridians encountered a system designed to restrict their political participation. Poll taxes, discriminatory administration and intimidation could make a constitutional right difficult or dangerous to exercise. The problem was not simply whether someone wished to vote. It was whether officials and neighbors would permit that person to enter the political community on equal terms.

The state historical marker for the Ocoee violence describes intimidation before the election, including Ku Klux Klan activity. It also identifies administrative obstacles: Black voters faced challenges and difficulties obtaining the paperwork associated with poll-tax requirements. Such details matter because election violence did not begin only when a weapon was fired. Threats and selective enforcement could prepare the ground by announcing whose participation would be resisted. [1]

Norman’s attempt to vote challenged that unequal order. He was not asking for an extraordinary privilege. He was trying to participate in an election. When his effort was met with exclusion and violence, the dispute exposed the distance between constitutional language and local power.

From the polling place to July Perry’s home

After Norman was turned away, armed white men sought him at Perry’s residence. Norman was no longer there. A gunfight followed; two white men were killed, and Perry was wounded. Perry was later taken to jail in Orlando. Instead of receiving protection and a lawful determination of events, he was taken by a lynch mob and killed. [1], [2]

Perry’s death was an act of extrajudicial violence. That term means punishment carried out outside legal process. It is especially important here because the presence of a jail did not ensure the protection of a prisoner. The failure was not merely an absence of government. Public authority existed, but it did not keep him safe from a mob.

Accounts of the initial confrontation have been shaped by unequal records and by the interests of those who produced them. Historians must compare testimony, contemporary reporting and later research rather than assume every surviving account is neutral. Uncertainty about particular moments does not erase what the broader evidence establishes: a Black community was attacked and expelled.

Destruction after Election Day

On November 3, a large white mob attacked Ocoee’s northern Black neighborhood, often called the Northern Quarters. Buildings burned, people were killed and residents fled. Black residents in the southern neighborhood were also forced out in the aftermath. The destruction reached beyond individuals directly involved in the first confrontation. Families lost their security because they belonged to the community the attackers targeted. [1]

The exact number of people killed remains uncertain. Responsible accounts should not turn estimates into an established total. The state’s research draws upon records that are incomplete and sometimes contradictory. Naming that uncertainty is part of documenting the massacre honestly; it is not a reason to minimize its scale or consequences.

Population records reveal another dimension of the loss. The historical marker identifies 495 Black residents in Ocoee in the 1920 census and describes the community’s removal. Behind that number were households, work, worship, neighborhood ties and plans for the future. Expulsion meant losing an entire local network as well as a place to sleep.

Property, memory and the long aftermath

Violence can transfer wealth without a formal transaction freely agreed to by both parties. A family escaping danger cannot negotiate over land in ordinary circumstances. Documents concerning ownership and later sales therefore require attention to the coercion surrounding them. A signature or recorded transfer does not, by itself, explain whether people had a meaningful choice.

The Florida Legislature’s research office, OPPAGA, investigated the events using sources including census material, property records, testimony, newspapers and death records. That range illustrates why recovering this history is difficult. No single document contains the full story. A property record might identify a parcel, while testimony explains why its owners left. A newspaper might preserve a date while also repeating the prejudices of its time. [2]

The massacre also changed what future generations could remember locally. When residents are forced away, their stories may survive in dispersed families rather than in the public institutions of the town they left. Commemoration then becomes more than putting up a marker. It involves reconnecting descendants, records and places that violence separated.

Understanding cause without excusing violence

The immediate dispute concerned voting, but the attack was rooted in a wider system of racial domination. Describing it simply as a disagreement between two groups hides the unequal power involved. A mob’s collective punishment of Black residents was not a normal electoral contest. It was a violent effort to determine who could belong, own property and exercise political rights.

At the same time, the people who were targeted should not appear only as victims without agency. Norman attempted to vote. Perry had a home and standing in his community. Other residents worked, raised families and built institutions before they were driven away. Beginning their history only with the destruction gives the attackers too much control over the story.

Why it matters

Ocoee connects the history of voting rights with the histories of land, wealth, community and public responsibility. The right to cast a ballot is weakened when exercising it threatens a person’s home or life. The protection of that right therefore involves more than the mechanics of an election; it requires equal treatment and meaningful protection from intimidation.

November 2, 1920, deserves remembrance as the beginning of a documented campaign of racial violence, followed by destruction and displacement. Its importance lies both in what happened and in how carefully the story must be recovered. Florida’s history includes communities that survived, communities that grew and communities whose residents were forcibly prevented from remaining. Ocoee requires us to remember all three.

Classroom Connection

Reading Prompt

Track what occurred on November 2, what continued on November 3, and what lasted much longer.

Glossary

Disenfranchisement — denial or obstruction of voting rights; lynching — killing by a mob without legal trial; coercion — pressure or threats that remove meaningful choice; primary source — evidence created during or directly connected with the events studied.

Reading Check

1. What right was Mose Norman attempting to exercise? 2. Why does the article avoid giving a definite death toll?

Think and Discuss

How can violence against one household affect the rights of an entire community?

Short Writing Prompt

Explain how two different kinds of records could help researchers reconstruct the massacre.

Teacher Notes and Suggested Answers

1. Voting. 2. Surviving evidence does not establish a reliable exact total. Census and property records help establish residence and ownership; testimony and reporting help explain events and forced departure.
