Army Wagon Trains

In the General's Own Hand

Florida Learning Hub · Mini Article · Florida Frontier Defense & Military History

On November 25, 1836, General Thomas Sidney Jesup wrote in his own diary that, with “means of transportation being ready, and every necessary arrangement made,” he had issued orders for the Army to march the following morning. His next entry describes exactly what that readiness actually required: “the most active and continued exercises were made to have the baggage wagons loaded, the surplus baggage dispersed of, provisions cooked, and all things in supply.” Jesup's own handwritten words, preserved today by the State Archives of Florida, offer a rare, firsthand window into the wagon logistics that kept the Second Seminole War's armies moving.

This article continues Fort Pillar V's exploration of Florida's military roads and transportation, turning to the wagons themselves — the vehicles, the animals, and the men whose exhausting work kept Florida's fort network supplied.

“The most active and continued exercises were made to have the baggage wagons loaded, the surplus baggage dispersed of, provisions cooked, and all things in supply.”

The General Who Rebuilt the Wagon System

Thomas Sidney Jesup had already reshaped how the Army supplied itself years before he ever set foot in Florida. Appointed Quartermaster General of the United States Army in 1818, Jesup made significant, lasting changes to the structure, operations, and regulations of the Quartermaster's Department, organizing troop transportation and maintaining supply through careful record-keeping, business arrangements, and budget appropriations.

When the Second Seminole War broke out in 1835, Jesup brought that same organizational discipline directly to Florida's difficult wagon logistics, ordering fresh horses obtained for his wagons even when it meant, in his own diary's words, dismounting some of his own mounted troops to get them.

Not every post enjoyed Jesup's level of organizational support. Fort Lauderdale, a small outpost on the edge of the Everglades, depended on a single lieutenant who simultaneously served as “commissary, quartermaster, ordnance officer, and adjutant” — one officer managing the supply, transportation, and administrative burden for his remote post.

Twenty-Five Wagons for a Thousand Men

The wagons themselves followed a standardized design across the nineteenth-century Army. A typical wagon body measured roughly ten feet long and was pulled by teams of four to six mules or horses. The animals nearest the wagon were called the wheel pair, the middle animals the swing pair, and the front animals the lead pair.

A four-mule team could haul about a ton of freight; adding a second pair brought that closer to a ton and a half. National accounts of Civil War-era wagon logistics, describing a system that had already taken shape during Jesup's reforms decades earlier, note that supply officers typically calculated roughly twenty-five wagons to keep a thousand soldiers fed and equipped.

The men who drove these teams, known as teamsters, rode the left wheel mule rather than sitting on the wagon, reserving every available inch of wagon space for freight. Their work was grueling: navigating Florida's stump-filled trails and swallowing mud while caring for the draft animals and keeping the wagons in working order. One period observer described teamsters filling the air with profanity “addressed to their mules, individually or collectively” — an unglamorous but essential part of how Florida's fort network stayed supplied.

Why This Mattered for Florida

Army wagon trains represent the physical labor underlying every fort, road, and supply route in this pillar — the actual wagons, animals, and teamsters that carried food, ammunition, and equipment along Bellamy Road and through the district supply network. Jesup's own diary gives us a primary-source view of a system that otherwise disappears easily behind maps and fort names.

Understanding the wagon system completes the picture of how Florida's frontier military network functioned day to day: one loaded wagon and one exhausted mule team at a time.

Did You Know?

  • General Thomas Sidney Jesup's November 1836 diary survives today and describes baggage-wagon preparations before an actual Second Seminole War campaign march.
  • A standard Army wagon team's animals were named by position: wheel pair, swing pair, and lead pair.
  • At Fort Lauderdale, a single lieutenant managed the post's supply operation while also serving as commissary, quartermaster, ordnance officer, and adjutant.

Vocabulary

  • Quartermaster — a military officer responsible for supplying troops with food, equipment, transportation, and other necessities.
  • Teamster — a person who drives a team of draft animals pulling a wagon.
  • Baggage — the equipment, supplies, and personal belongings an army carries with it on campaign.
