When the Rails Reached Gainesville

How the Railroad Changed Interior Florida Forever

A railroad that had not yet arrived was the reason the town existed.

In the early 1850s Alachua County did something county seats rarely do: it moved. Newnansville, northeast of present-day Alachua, had been the county seat since 1828, apart from a gap from 1832 to 1839. But when the Florida Railroad planned a line from Fernandina to Cedar Key, the route ran several miles south of it. The Florida legislature authorized relocating the seat in December 1852, and in September 1854 the county commission announced it had secured a site for a new courthouse town along the planned route. They named it Gainesville.

A Town Built to Meet a Train

Newnansville had grown from a post office established in 1826. A state marker describes it as a commercial center on the Middle Florida frontier, at a junction of trails that included the Bellamy Road. Historian Susan Yelton reports that in 1830 its area counted 413 residents, 164 of them enslaved Black people. The usual explanation is that Gainesville honored General Edmund P. Gaines, who commanded U.S. forces in Florida in the early months of the Second Seminole War, though naming accounts rest on local histories.

The Florida Railroad reached Gainesville on February 1, 1859, according to the 2003 state marker “Gainesville’s Railroads,” and the Florida Railroad Museum likewise says by that date. The marker says that before the railroad travel was by sandy, swampy roads, and that rail access to the coast gave farmers and merchants a way to move goods. The line reached Cedar Key in March 1861. Alachua County’s cotton and corn farms, and the railroad itself, depended on enslaved labor.

Waldo and Archer

Northeast of Gainesville, the railroad created Waldo at the crossing of the new line and the older Bellamy Road. A state marker says a post office opened there in 1858 and the town was named for Dr. Benjamin Waldo, a physician and former South Carolina legislator. Local accounts say David Levy Yulee, who chose names for railroad stations, honored him, but the link between Waldo and Yulee has been described as circumstantial. The marker says Waldo remained a major rail junction until the railroad’s shops and headquarters moved away in 1929.

Southwest of Gainesville, the railroad reshaped an existing settlement known as Deer Hammock. Sources disagree on when it began. The state marker says that when the railroad arrived in 1859 the town was renamed Archer and its post office opened, after James T. Archer, a Tallahassee attorney who became Florida’s first Secretary of State after statehood. Quaker settlers arrived in the late 1870s and planted orange groves, and freezes in 1886 and 1894–95 killed the trees.

Cotton Wood

Archer lay near Yulee’s Cotton Wood plantation. In May 1865, as the Confederacy collapsed, a party escorting Confederate government papers and Jefferson Davis’s personal baggage stopped nearby. Accounts differ about whether any government funds were also hidden, and this article treats the buried items as papers and personal effects. A state marker says they were later moved to Waldo, where a Union detachment seized them. The episode is told in A Midnight Errand at Cotton Wood.

Left Behind

Newnansville shows the other side of the pattern. A state marker ties its decline to the arrival of steamers in 1876 and the extension of the railroad to Micanopy in the early 1880s, and the town was abandoned by about 1900. A new town, Alachua, grew a mile or so away at a railroad station. In each case the railroad shaped where a town would stand: Gainesville was a county seat relocated to the planned line, Waldo grew at a crossing, and Archer was renamed and built up around the depot.

Quick Facts

County-seat move

Authorized December 1852; site announced September 6, 1854 (Yelton)

Why Newnansville lost it

The planned railroad route passed several miles south

First train into Gainesville

February 1, 1859 (Florida Heritage marker)

Line completed to Cedar Key

March 1861

Waldo

Post office 1858 at the railroad–Bellamy Road crossing; named for Dr. Benjamin Waldo (state marker)

Archer

Formerly Deer Hammock; renamed 1859 for James T. Archer

Newnansville

Abandoned by about 1900; Alachua grew nearby

Cotton Wood

Yulee’s plantation near Archer; Davis’s baggage and Confederate papers, May 1865

Gainesville’s name

Usually said to honor General Edmund P. Gaines

Did You Know?

* Gainesville was laid out for a railroad that did not reach it for about five years.

* Waldo grew where the Florida Railroad crossed the Bellamy Road.

* Archer was named for Florida’s first Secretary of State after statehood.

Vocabulary

County seat: the town where a county’s government meets.

Depot: a railroad station.

Junction: a place where rail lines or roads meet or cross.
