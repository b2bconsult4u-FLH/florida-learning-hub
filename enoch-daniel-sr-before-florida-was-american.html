Enoch Daniel Sr.: Before Florida Was American

A Pioneer of Elotchaway and the Alachua Frontier

Florida Learning Hub · Pioneer Families Mini

When Enoch Daniel Sr. entered Florida, the peninsula was still Spanish territory. The United States had no Florida Territory, Gainesville did not exist, and the interior Alachua country remained a contested frontier of prairie, hammock, Native towns, scattered settlers, traders, soldiers, and shifting political authority. By 1813, Daniel was among the Americans entering that world. A year later, surviving records of Elotchaway specifically named “Enoch Daniel, Sr.”

His arrival coincided with one of the least-known chapters of Florida history. Beginning in 1812, American-supported Patriots, aided at different times by U.S. forces and Georgia volunteers, entered Spanish East Florida in an effort to detach the province from Spain and bring it under American control.

Enoch Daniel Sr. was in Florida before Florida was American.

Enoch Daniel Comes South

The Florida State Genealogical Society's Pioneer Database records Enoch Daniel Sr. as born about 1760 and arriving in Florida in 1813, associated with Alachua. That places him in Spanish East Florida eight years before Spain formally transferred the territory to the United States in 1821.

No primary record naming Daniel in 1812 has yet been located, so the stronger publication-grade statement is that he was in Spanish East Florida by 1813 and was specifically named in Elotchaway records in 1814.

Elotchaway, 1814

The strongest early documentary anchor for Enoch Sr. comes from records discussed by historian T. Frederick Davis in “Elotchaway, East Florida, 1814.” Those surviving materials identify “Enoch Daniel, Sr.” among the American settlers of the interior East Florida settlement effort.

The “Sr.” designation is important because it distinguishes him from his son, Enoch Daniel Jr., and demonstrates that the family already spanned two generations on the Florida frontier during the Spanish period.

Building a Life on the Interior Frontier

Life in the Elotchaway and Alachua interior in the 1810s meant distance from established coastal centers such as St. Augustine, difficult transportation, dependence on agriculture and livestock, and an unsettled political environment where Spanish and American authority overlapped and competed. The region was also part of a Native frontier whose control remained very much in dispute.

Daniel was not arriving in an organized American territory. He was putting down roots in a place where sovereignty itself was unsettled.

The Daniel–Dell Pioneer Network

The Daniel and Dell families repeatedly appear together in early northern Florida records, and genealogical evidence connects them by marriage. An 1818 record involving Abraham Daniel, an Enoch Daniel, and members of the Dell family appears in connection with frontier activity near the St. Marys River during the First Seminole War period.

Because that record does not identify the Enoch as “Sr.” or “Jr.,” and Enoch Jr. was old enough to have appeared independently, the evidence does not conclusively establish which man was involved. What can be said confidently is that the Daniels belonged to an interconnected pioneer network rather than existing as an isolated family.

Florida Becomes American

Spain formally transferred Florida to the United States in 1821. For Enoch Daniel Sr., the change transformed the government around him without necessarily changing where he lived: a man who had entered Spanish territory was now a resident of the new American territory.

The Daniels of Alachua

The 1830 federal census for Florida Territory separately lists “Daniel, Enoch, Jr.” and “Daniel, Enoch, Sr.” in Alachua County. That record confirms Enoch Sr.'s continued Florida presence and establishes Enoch Jr. as a separate adult head of household.

A December 25, 1830 petition from Alachua County residents preserved in the Territorial Papers of the United States lists Abram Daniel, Enoch Daniel, Bennett M. Dell, Simon Dell, and other pioneer settlers. The petition places the Daniels within the civic and political life of territorial Florida.

The Daniels Reach the McMullen Line

Enoch Daniel Sr.'s descendants eventually connected directly to the McMullen family of the Pinellas peninsula. The documented line runs through Enoch Daniel Jr.; Rebecca Elizabeth Daniels Waterson; Robert James Waterson; Alice Jane Waterson McMullen; William Eli McMullen Sr.; and William Eli McMullen II.

That line supports the statement used in Florida Learning Hub author notes: Wm. E. McMullen II is a seventh-generation Floridian whose direct family line is documented in territorial Florida by 1830.

Why This Mattered for Florida

Enoch Daniel Sr. represents a generation of settlers whose Florida story began before U.S. territorial government existed. His record links Spanish East Florida, the Patriot era, the Alachua frontier, territorial settlement, and later pioneer families into one continuous story.

His son and descendants carried that story forward into the Seminole War era, southwest Florida, cattle country, Charlotte Harbor, and eventually Pinellas County. The Daniel family's documented Florida presence begins in 1813 — before Florida became American territory.

Did You Know?

  • Enoch Daniel Sr. was documented in Florida eight years before the 1821 transfer from Spain to the United States.
  • Both Enoch Daniel Sr. and Enoch Daniel Jr. appear separately in the 1830 Alachua County census.
  • The Daniel and Dell families appear together repeatedly in early north Florida records.

Vocabulary

  • Spanish East Florida — the eastern portion of Spanish-controlled Florida before the 1821 transfer to the United States.
  • Elotchaway — an early name associated with the Alachua interior and prairie region.
  • Territorial period — Florida's period as a U.S. territory from 1821 until statehood in 1845.
