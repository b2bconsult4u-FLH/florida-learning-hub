Traveling the Florida Frontier

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

What It Felt Like to Move Through Pioneer Florida — the Full Human Experience of a World in Motion

Journals, letters and recollections are the best guides, and few of them survive for any one route.

What was it like to travel through pioneer Florida? Not in the technical sense of road surface, ferry toll or daily mileage, but in the human sense. What did a traveler see, smell, hear and feel in the 1840s and 1850s? These questions do not have single answers. Every traveler’s experience was different, shaped by season, weather, route and company. Journals, letters and recollections are the best guides, and few survive for any one route, so this account marks where the evidence is thin.

The Landscape of the Road

Longleaf pine flatwoods, with an open canopy over wiregrass and other low plants, covered large areas of the upland, so travelers moving through them were exposed to sun and sky. Camp at night would have been loud with frogs, owls and insects, and mosquitoes and biting flies were an unavoidable part of travel in warm months. Springs, cypress swamps, pine woods and the coast differ in look and smell, and a traveler who knew the country could read them.

People on the Road

A traveler would not necessarily be alone. Mail carriers connected scattered settlements; historian Richard J. Stanaback found that Florida had almost no communication links in 1821 and about fifty post offices and 2,920 miles of post roads by 1845. Cattle drovers moved herds along the same roads, and a wagon driver might have to wait for a herd to pass. Itinerant peddlers were a feature of nineteenth-century frontiers generally. Methodist circuit riders also served scattered communities; a Florida Heritage Marker for Dell’s Courthouse in Newnansville states that Methodist missionaries were active in northeast Florida by 1822.

Hospitality and the Journey

Travelers in sparsely settled country depended on households along the road for water, a meal or a place to sleep. No source located shows a Florida law or custom requiring this, and accounts by individual travelers would be needed to say how common such help was.

A move by wagon could take weeks and demanded physical labor from the whole family. Many families probably remembered the journey as one of the memorable events of their lives.

Whose Roads Were These?

The pioneer-era traveler is only one part of the history of movement through Florida. Seminole and Miccosukee people lived in and traveled through the same country before, during and after the pioneer era, and they remain in Florida today. The federal military roads were built in the context of the Seminole Wars and federal removal policy, and a full account of travel in this period must include that context.

Quick Facts

Who traveled

Settlers, soldiers, mail carriers, traders, cattle drovers, surveyors and circuit preachers

Wagon distance

10 to 15 miles a day is the National Park Service figure for the western trails; no Florida figure located

Post offices

About 50 by 1845 (Stanaback)

Post roads

2,920 miles by 1845 (Stanaback)

Methodist missionaries

Active in northeast Florida by 1822 (Dell’s Courthouse marker)

Evidence

Few traveler journals or letters located for the Georgia–Florida roads

Hospitality

Did You Know?

* Florida had about fifty post offices by 1845.

* Methodist missionaries were active in northeast Florida by 1822.

* Few traveler diaries survive for any one frontier route.

Vocabulary

Circuit rider: a minister who traveled a route to serve scattered congregations.

Itinerant: traveling from place to place.

Drover: a person who drives livestock to market.

Discussion Questions
