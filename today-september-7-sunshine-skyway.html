The Sunshine Skyway Opens Across Tampa Bay

September 7, 1954 — Florida opened a highway across the mouth of Tampa Bay.

For motorists traveling between St. Petersburg and communities to the south, the new Sunshine Skyway fundamentally changed the geography of Florida's Gulf Coast.

Before the bridge, Tampa Bay was a formidable obstacle. Travelers could take a ferry across the lower bay or make the much longer automobile trip around the water through Tampa. The new crossing connected the Pinellas Peninsula directly with Manatee County and created a transportation route that would eventually become part of Interstate 275.

The original Sunshine Skyway looked nothing like the great yellow cable-stayed bridge Floridians recognize today.

It was a sprawling system of five bridges and six fills, stretching across miles of open water. At its center stood a towering steel cantilever bridge that carried motorists high above the shipping channel leading between Tampa Bay and the Gulf of Mexico.

For Florida in 1954, it was an extraordinary engineering achievement.

But the history of the Sunshine Skyway would eventually become much more than the story of a bridge.

It would become a story of growth, engineering, catastrophe, loss, reconstruction—and the lessons Florida learned from one of the worst bridge disasters in its history.

Before the Skyway

The problem was obvious to anyone looking at a map.

St. Petersburg occupied the southern portion of the Pinellas Peninsula. Bradenton, Palmetto and Sarasota lay to the south.

Between them was lower Tampa Bay.

For decades, motorists traveling between those areas had two practical choices: drive around the bay or cross it by ferry.

The Bee Line Ferry provided an important connection between Pinellas and Manatee counties. Ferries could transport automobiles across the water, saving travelers from the long drive through Tampa.

But ferries had limitations. They carried only so many automobiles. Travelers sometimes waited for space. Weather and rough water could interfere with service. And as Florida's population and automobile traffic increased, a ferry system could not provide the kind of continuous highway connection the growing Gulf Coast increasingly needed.

By the end of World War II, Florida was entering a period of extraordinary change.

Returning servicemen were establishing families. Tourism was expanding. Air conditioning was making year-round life in Florida more attractive. Automobile ownership was increasing. New subdivisions were spreading across the state.

Florida needed roads capable of serving the state it was becoming.

A permanent crossing of lower Tampa Bay moved from an ambitious idea toward reality.

Building a Road Across Tampa Bay

The Sunshine Skyway was not simply one enormous bridge.

Engineers created an entire highway system across the bay.

Causeways and fills carried motorists across shallower sections of water. Lower bridges crossed other portions of the route. At the principal shipping channel, however, engineers faced a much greater challenge.

Tampa Bay was—and remains—a major commercial port.

Ocean-going ships needed to travel between the Gulf of Mexico and ports inside the bay.

The highway therefore had to climb high enough above the water for ships to pass underneath.

The solution was the massive steel cantilever span that became the defining feature of the original Sunshine Skyway.

From the flat Florida landscape, motorists approached a roadway that suddenly began climbing toward the sky.

The bridge rose above the water. The bay opened around them. Then vehicles passed through the great steel framework of the central span before descending toward the opposite shore.

For generations of Floridians, crossing the Skyway became an experience in itself.

An Opening-Day Photograph

One of the most remarkable records of the original bridge survives in the State Archives of Florida.

Florida Memory identifies this photograph as RC06695 and dates it precisely: September 7, 1954.

It was taken from the Manatee County side of Tampa Bay and shows the newly completed Sunshine Skyway winding across the water. Florida Memory's archival description identifies the original crossing as a system of five bridges and six fills.

Look Closely at the Photograph

The photograph explains the original Skyway better than almost any description can.

The enormous steel high span immediately attracts the eye.

But look beyond it.

The roadway seems almost to float across Tampa Bay.

Long, low approaches stretch across the water before climbing toward the main span. Automobiles appear tiny against the scale of the crossing.

And in the foreground is something especially important.

Traffic.

Florida was becoming an automobile state.

The Sunshine Skyway was being constructed for the Florida that would emerge during the second half of the twentieth century—a state of highways, suburban development, tourism and enormous population growth.

The bridge did not merely respond to that transformation.

It helped make it possible.

The Bridge Changed the Map

A major bridge does something that is easy to underestimate.

It changes distance.

St. Petersburg and Manatee County had always been geographically close.

Tampa Bay made them functionally farther apart.

The Skyway changed that.

A continuous highway now connected the two sides of lower Tampa Bay.

Travel between Pinellas, Manatee and Sarasota counties became easier. Tourists moving along Florida's Gulf Coast gained a direct route. Commercial traffic gained another north-south connection.

Eventually the corridor became part of Interstate 275 and U.S. 19.

Places that had always been separated by water became connected by pavement.

That is why major transportation projects can have effects far beyond the concrete and steel used to construct them.

Roads influence where people live. They influence where businesses locate. They affect tourism. They influence land values and development. They change which communities people consider close enough to visit, work in or commute between.

The Sunshine Skyway altered the human geography of Florida's Gulf Coast.

Florida Outgrew the First Skyway

There was one major problem with the original bridge.

It carried only two lanes of traffic.

Florida's population continued growing rapidly during the 1950s and 1960s.

Traffic increased with it.

Eventually, Florida decided that the Skyway needed additional capacity.

A second bridge was constructed parallel to the original.

The similar second high-level span opened in 1971, allowing the crossing to operate with separate bridges for opposing directions of traffic.

For several years, the twin bridges carried traffic safely across Tampa Bay.

Thousands of large ships also passed underneath.

Florida State Parks notes that between the opening of the original bridge and 1980, thousands of ships and boats traveled beneath the Skyway without incident.

Then came the morning of May 9, 1980.

The Day the Skyway Fell

A violent thunderstorm swept across Tampa Bay.

Visibility deteriorated dramatically.

The freighter Summit Venture was navigating toward the bridge.

During the storm, the vessel moved out of the safe shipping channel.

At approximately 7:30 that morning, the ship struck a major support of the southbound Skyway.

The impact was catastrophic.

Approximately 1,200 feet of bridge collapsed.

Vehicles approaching the damaged span could not immediately see that the highway ahead of them had disappeared.

Six automobiles and a Greyhound bus plunged approximately 150 feet into Tampa Bay.

Thirty-five people died.

It became one of the darkest days in Florida transportation history.

A bridge that had symbolized connection and progress suddenly became the scene of unimaginable tragedy.

Fourteen Inches From the Edge

One automobile did not fall.

Richard Hornbuckle was traveling south with three friends when he realized something was terribly wrong ahead.

He hit the brakes.

The Buick stopped.

According to the account preserved by Florida State Parks, the car came to rest only 14 inches from the broken edge of the bridge.

Beyond the hood of the automobile was open air.

The roadway was gone.

Photographs of vehicles stopped before the missing span became some of the most unforgettable images in modern Florida history.

They also made the engineering problem impossible to ignore.

The old Skyway had been built to allow ships to pass beneath it.

But its critical supports remained vulnerable if a large vessel left the shipping channel.

Florida now had to decide what came next.

Rebuilding Was Not Enough

Florida could have attempted simply to restore the damaged bridge.

Instead, the disaster forced engineers and government officials to reconsider the entire crossing.

The question was no longer: How do we build a bridge across Tampa Bay?

Florida had already solved that problem.

The new question was: How do we build a bridge across a major shipping channel that can better withstand the possibility of a vessel leaving that channel?

The answer produced a radically different Sunshine Skyway.

The Bridge We Know Today

Construction of the replacement bridge began in the 1980s.

The old steel cantilever design gave way to a cable-stayed bridge.

Two massive towers rose over Tampa Bay.

A single plane of great yellow cables supported the main roadway.

The central span provided a much wider navigational opening.

The bridge's principal supports were also protected by substantial structures designed to prevent a ship from directly striking them.

This was one of the fundamental lessons of the Summit Venture disaster.

The new bridge would not simply stand above ships.

Its design would account for what could happen if a ship went where it was never supposed to go.

An Engineering Landmark

The modern Sunshine Skyway is itself an extraordinary structure.

FDOT's historic bridge documentation records the bridge as approximately 29,040 feet long, with a 1,200-foot main span. Construction began in 1982, and the new bridge opened to traffic in 1987.

Its yellow cables quickly became recognizable throughout Florida.

The design transformed a transportation structure into a landmark.

For motorists approaching from either direction, the towers appear long before the roadway reaches them.

The bridge climbs.

The cables spread outward.

The horizon opens across Tampa Bay.

Then the highway rises high above one of Florida's most important shipping channels.

The bridge is functional.

But it is also unmistakably Florida.

The Old Skyway Did Not Completely Disappear

After the replacement bridge opened, Florida faced another question.

What should be done with the surviving portions of the old bridges?

The center spans were eventually demolished in 1992.

But the long approaches extending into Tampa Bay remained.

Instead of removing them completely, Florida transformed them.

In 1994, portions of the old Skyway became Skyway Fishing Pier State Park.

A highway built for automobiles became a destination for fishermen.

Even material from the demolished portions of the bridge found another purpose.

Florida State Parks records that recovered debris was placed in Tampa Bay to create artificial reefs alongside the piers.

So pieces of the original Sunshine Skyway survive in several forms.

Some remain above the water as fishing piers.

Others rest beneath Tampa Bay as habitat.

And photographs such as RC06695 preserve what the entire crossing looked like when it was new.

Three Generations of Sunshine Skyway

1954 — The Original Skyway

A two-lane crossing finally created a permanent highway connection between Pinellas and Manatee counties across lower Tampa Bay.

1971 — The Parallel Span

Florida's tremendous growth required additional highway capacity, and a second high-level bridge was constructed beside the first.

1987 — The Modern Sunshine Skyway

The Summit Venture catastrophe forced Florida to rethink the crossing. The resulting cable-stayed bridge incorporated lessons about navigation, vessel impact and protection of critical bridge supports.

Each bridge represents the Florida of its time.

The first represents postwar optimism and expansion.

The second represents explosive population and automobile growth.

The third represents engineering shaped by tragedy.

More Than a Bridge

For someone who has crossed the Sunshine Skyway many times, it can become easy to think of it simply as part of the highway.

But bridges like this deserve a second look.

The Sunshine Skyway represents an extraordinary concentration of Florida history in one place.

Transportation. Tourism. Population growth. Engineering. Maritime commerce. Disaster. Environmental reuse. Public works. And human loss.

All of those stories intersect across the same stretch of Tampa Bay.

The bridge Floridians drive today exists because generations before us tried to solve the same basic problem:

How do we get across this water?

The answer changed as Florida changed.

First came ferries. Then came the two-lane Skyway. Then twin bridges. Then catastrophe. Then the great cable-stayed bridge.

September 7 in Florida History

Look again at the photograph from September 7, 1954.

The bridge is new.

Automobiles are crossing the water.

The steel span rises confidently above Tampa Bay.

Nobody in that photograph could know what would happen twenty-six years later.

They could not know that another bridge would be built beside it.

They could not know that the Summit Venture would strike that later span during a thunderstorm.

They could not know that thirty-five people would die.

And they could not imagine the enormous yellow cable-stayed bridge that would eventually rise nearby.

On that September day, Floridians simply knew that something remarkable had happened.

A road had been built across Tampa Bay.

It shortened distances.

It connected communities.

It changed travel along Florida's Gulf Coast.

And its successors would become among the most recognizable structures in the state.

The modern Sunshine Skyway is therefore more than the bridge that replaced the original.

It is a monument to what Florida built, what Florida lost, and what Florida learned.
