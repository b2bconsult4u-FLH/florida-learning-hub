Today in Florida History · September 8

St. Augustine Is Established

September 8, 1565 — Pedro Menéndez de Avilés formally claimed Florida and began establishing the Spanish settlement of San Agustín beside the Timucua community of Seloy. The settlement survived, making this one of the most consequential dates in Florida history.

St. Augustine predates Jamestown by forty-two years and Plymouth by fifty-five years. But its founding story is more complicated than an explorer stepping ashore. Menéndez entered an inhabited Indigenous world and an international struggle between Spain and France for control of Florida and the Atlantic.

Florida Was Already Someone's Home

The history of St. Augustine did not begin with Menéndez. Indigenous peoples had lived throughout Florida for thousands of years. The area where the Spaniards established their first settlement was associated with Timucua-speaking peoples, whose communities occupied much of northern Florida and southeastern Georgia.

Menéndez arrived at an existing community: Seloy. The Spanish initially lived among its people and relied on Indigenous food, pottery and local knowledge. Historical and archaeological research indicates that a large Timucua structure at Seloy was adapted for the Spaniards' first defensive position, although current archaeological work cautions that the exact physical form of that first fort remains uncertain.

Why Spain Came to Florida

Spain had claimed Florida more than half a century earlier. Juan Ponce de León reached Florida in 1513, and other expeditions followed. By the 1560s Spain faced a new problem: French Protestants had established Fort Caroline near the St. Johns River.

The French settlement stood uncomfortably close to routes used by Spanish shipping. King Philip II charged Menéndez with establishing Spain's position in Florida and eliminating the French foothold. His expedition therefore combined colonization, military strategy, imperial rivalry and religion.

August 28 — The Name Comes First

Menéndez's fleet sighted Florida on August 28, 1565, the feast day of Saint Augustine of Hippo. The settlement that followed became known as San Agustín.

But sighting Florida and establishing St. Augustine were not the same event. The University of Florida's Menéndez chronology records that on September 7 Captains Morales and Patiño landed with thirty men and began defensive entrenchments. Then came September 8. Menéndez formally claimed Florida and began unloading two of his ships.

September 8, 1565

Imagine the scene without the modern city: no Castillo de San Marcos, Bridge of Lions, hotels, paved streets or seawall. There was water, marsh, forest and an established Timucua landscape. Ships rested offshore while small boats carried people, weapons, tools, food and supplies toward land.

Among the expedition was Father Francisco López de Mendoza Grajales, chaplain to Menéndez. Father López celebrated a Mass on September 8, an event that marked the formal establishment of the town.

An Archival Interpretation of the Landing

Florida Memory preserves a historic postcard depicting the September 8 landing and Mass. The postcard is a later artistic interpretation—not an eyewitness image of 1565—and is valuable as evidence of how later generations commemorated St. Augustine's founding.

A Mass — And a Meal

The ceremony did not end with the Mass. Historical interpretation of the event records a communal meal involving the Spanish newcomers and local Indigenous people. This has produced the famous claim that America's first Thanksgiving occurred in Florida.

There is a legitimate historical basis for discussing the September 8 gathering as a thanksgiving feast, but the word “first” depends on definition. Indigenous peoples had traditions of ceremony and giving thanks long before Europeans arrived, and other thanksgiving observances occurred in the Spanish Americas.

The historically important point needs no exaggeration: on September 8, 1565, the Spanish celebrated their arrival and establishment at St. Augustine with a religious observance and communal meal decades before the familiar Plymouth story.

Who Came With Menéndez?

The expedition was not simply a band of soldiers. University of Florida historical archaeology describes a group of roughly 800 colonists, including twenty-six women and people of African heritage. Both free and enslaved Africans became part of early Spanish Florida.

Spanish, Indigenous and African lives were therefore intersecting in Florida in the sixteenth century, long before the later English colonies that dominate many traditional accounts of early American history.

The French Were Still There

Even while St. Augustine was being established, Fort Caroline remained nearby. French commander Jean Ribault attempted to attack the new Spanish position, but severe weather scattered his fleet along Florida's Atlantic coast.

Menéndez seized the opportunity. He led Spanish soldiers north overland through difficult terrain and captured Fort Caroline on September 20, 1565, renaming it San Mateo.

Matanzas

Survivors of Ribault's wrecked fleet attempted to return north. Groups encountered Menéndez south of St. Augustine and many surrendered Frenchmen were killed. The place became known as Matanzas—a name associated with slaughter or killing in Spanish.

The name survives today in Matanzas Bay, the Matanzas River, Matanzas Inlet and Fort Matanzas National Monument. A modern Florida place-name therefore preserves the memory of the brutal imperial and religious struggle surrounding St. Augustine's birth.

St. Augustine's founding was an extraordinary achievement of colonization and survival. It was also part of a violent contest for control of Florida. Both truths belong in the history.

Survival Was Far From Certain

Establishing St. Augustine did not guarantee that it would survive. Food could become scarce. Hurricanes threatened ships and supplies. Disease, warfare and deteriorating relations with Indigenous communities threatened the colony.

The first settlement itself did not remain in one place. Relations between the Spaniards and Seloy's people worsened rapidly. By 1566 the Spaniards moved across the bay to Anastasia Island. Later they moved again.

The important point is continuity. The community survived.

A City That Refused to Disappear

Over the centuries St. Augustine endured war, attack, fire, hurricanes and political upheaval. Spain controlled Florida. Britain controlled Florida. Spain regained it. Then Florida became a territory of the United States.

The city's architecture, population, economy and political allegiance changed, but the settlement established in 1565 continued. That continuity is what makes St. Augustine exceptional.

Before Jamestown — Before Plymouth

Jamestown was established in Virginia in 1607. St. Augustine had already existed for forty-two years.

The Mayflower reached New England in 1620. By then St. Augustine was fifty-five years old.

A child born during St. Augustine's founding generation could have become a grandparent before the Pilgrims arrived at Plymouth. That is one of the clearest ways to understand the depth of Florida history.

The Castillo Came Much Later

Visitors today associate historic St. Augustine with the massive stone walls of the Castillo de San Marcos. Menéndez never saw it. Construction did not begin until 1672.

For more than a century before the Castillo existed, St. Augustine relied upon earlier wooden fortifications and defensive works. By the time construction began on the coquina fortress, the settlement had already existed for more than a hundred years.

Archaeology Brings 1565 Back to Life

Much of the earliest St. Augustine disappeared above ground. Wood decayed, buildings burned, shorelines changed and later development covered earlier sites.

Archaeological work on the grounds of today's Fountain of Youth Archaeological Park and the nearby Mission Nombre de Dios area has identified evidence associated with the Menéndez settlement. Pottery, beads, food remains, Spanish and Indigenous objects, and structural evidence help reconstruct the place where the enduring European colony began.

September 8 in Florida History

It is difficult to identify many single dates with greater importance to Florida history than September 8, 1565.

Menéndez formally claimed Florida. Spanish colonists unloaded their ships. A Catholic Mass marked the establishment of the settlement. Spanish newcomers entered the existing Timucua world of Seloy. Indigenous, European and African lives became part of the earliest St. Augustine story.

The city that grew from that foothold would outlive empires. Its flags would change. Its fortifications would change. Its streets would change.

But St. Augustine endured.

The Florida Story Begins Earlier Than Many Americans Realize

September 8 challenges the way American history is often remembered. The history of enduring European settlement in what became the United States does not begin only with English colonies in Virginia or Massachusetts.

There is an older chapter.

It is Spanish.

It is Indigenous.

It is African.

And it is Floridian.

That chapter begins among the marshes and waterways of northeast Florida, where Pedro Menéndez de Avilés and his colonists established themselves beside the Timucua community of Seloy.

September 8, 1565.

St. Augustine had begun.

And 461 years later, the city is still there.
