Florida’s Cow Cavalry

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

The Cowmen Who Became Soldiers to Keep an Army Fed

Soldiers elsewhere ate because Florida cattlemen got the beef to them.

When Vicksburg fell in July 1863, the Union took the Mississippi River and cut off one of the Confederacy’s main sources of beef. Confederate quartermasters turned to Florida, the least populous Confederate state, where hundreds of thousands of half-wild cattle roamed the peninsula. The cowhunters, ranchers and drovers who did the job became known as the Cow Cavalry.

A Captain’s Letter to Richmond

The unit owed its existence to Captain James McKay, a Tampa cattle shipper and former ship captain who had been appointed a Confederate commissary agent. According to a study of the Tampa Robles family that cites the letter, McKay wrote in March 1864 to Secretary of War James Seddon proposing a unit to gather Florida’s herds, guard them from Union raids and drive them north. The proposal was approved, and the result was the 1st Florida Special Cavalry Battalion, also called the Cattle Guard Battalion.

Command went to Charles J. Munnerlyn, a Georgia lawyer and former Confederate congressman. The battalion grew to nine companies and roughly nine hundred men. One company, led by Captain John T. Lesley, formed at Ichepucksassa, in the Cork area of today’s Plant City. The battalion patrolled a line about three hundred miles long, from upper Lafayette County to the Lake Okeechobee region.

Who Rode in the Cow Cavalry

Many of the men were the kind the regular army did not want: too young, or too old, for the front. They did not need infantry drill. They needed men who could read cattle, ride for days and improvise, and Florida’s cowhunting families had passed those skills down for generations. Captain Francis A. Hendry led a company and later became one of South Florida’s leading ranchers and the namesake of Hendry County. James McKay Jr. led another.

Protecting the Herds

Union naval outposts launched raids inland to capture or scatter cattle, and the Union also targeted salt works, since salt was needed to preserve meat. The battalion kept beef moving north for more than a year, driving herds to railheads in Georgia and South Carolina and sometimes as far as Charleston. Fort Meade on the Peace River served as a key staging point. Historians caution against overstating the result: with few railroads and drives of three to four hundred miles, Florida’s beef never fully met the Confederacy’s expectations.

The Battle of Fort Myers

Fort Myers, abandoned in 1858 after the Seminole Wars, was reoccupied by Union troops in December 1863, though some accounts say January 1864. It became a refuge for escaped enslaved people, Unionists and Confederate deserters, at times holding more than four hundred people. The garrison included the Union 2nd Florida Cavalry, a company from New York and companies of the 2nd U.S. Colored Infantry.

In February 1865 about 275 men from three companies of the battalion, under Major William Footman, advanced on the fort partly to end its threat to Confederate cattle operations. They demanded surrender on February 20, the date given by the state historical marker and by historian Irvin Solomon. Union Captain James Doyle refused, and after hours of artillery and musket fire the attack was repelled with light casualties. The engagement is often called the southernmost land battle of the Civil War, a claim sometimes disputed because of Palmito Ranch, Texas.

What the Cow Cavalry Left Behind

The unit fought no battle remembered beside Gettysburg, and most general histories omit it. Within Florida it is remembered for the supply line it protected. Markers in Plant City and at Hillsborough County Veterans Memorial Park have commemorated the battalion. The Confederate victory at Olustee in February 1864 helped keep open the roads and rail lines the battalion later depended on.

Quick Facts

Official name

1st Florida Special Cavalry Battalion (Cattle Guard Battalion)

Formed

Spring 1864, after McKay’s March 1864 proposal

Commander

Charles J. Munnerlyn

Size

Nine companies, roughly 900 men

Patrol line

About 300 miles, from Lafayette County to Lake Okeechobee

Who served

Cattlemen and cowhunters, many too young or too old for regular service

Mission

Gather and guard cattle and drive them north to Confederate armies

Cattle, 1860 census

About 388,060 head in Florida

Key engagement

Fort Myers, February 20, 1865

Did You Know?

  • The unit had two names, the Cow Cavalry and the Cattle Guard Battalion, for the same battalion.
  • At times more than four hundred people sheltered inside Fort Myers.
  • Captain Francis A. Hendry, who led a company, is the namesake of Hendry County.

Vocabulary

Commissary: the department that supplies an army with food.

Drover: a person who drives herds of cattle over long distances.

Railhead: the end of a railway line, where goods are loaded for shipment.
