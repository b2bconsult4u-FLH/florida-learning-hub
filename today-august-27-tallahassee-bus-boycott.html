Today in Florida History • August 27

1956: Tallahassee Bus Boycott Leaders Arrested for Keeping the Carpool Moving

On August 27, 1956, Tallahassee police arrested Rev. C.K. Steele and Rev. Daniel B. Speed for operating a carpool service without a license. The carpool was not an ordinary transportation business. It was helping Black residents continue a boycott of Tallahassee's segregated city buses.

What Happened?

The Tallahassee Bus Boycott began after Florida A&M University students Wilhelmina Jakes and Carrie Patterson were arrested in May 1956 following a confrontation over segregated seating on a city bus. The protest quickly grew beyond the university campus, and Tallahassee's Black community organized the Inter-Civic Council to sustain the boycott.

One of the boycott's biggest practical problems was transportation. People still needed to get to work, stores, churches, schools, and appointments without riding the buses. Boycott organizers responded by creating a coordinated carpool.

Rev. C.K. Steele and Rev. Daniel B. Speed became key leaders in that effort. On August 27, Tallahassee police arrested them for operating the carpool without a license. The arrests were part of a broader effort by local authorities to disrupt the transportation network that allowed the boycott to continue.

Why It Matters

The arrest illustrates an often-overlooked part of successful protest movements: logistics. A boycott could not survive on speeches and public meetings alone. Participants needed a practical alternative to the service they were refusing to use.

The Tallahassee carpool gave residents that alternative. Even after arrests and legal pressure, the boycott continued. The campaign ultimately helped bring an end to segregated seating on Tallahassee's city buses.

Florida Learning Hub Connection

This event connects Florida's civil-rights history with Florida A&M University, Tallahassee, nonviolent protest, local government, transportation, and the broader struggle to dismantle segregation. It also demonstrates how ordinary transportation decisions became part of a major constitutional and social conflict.
