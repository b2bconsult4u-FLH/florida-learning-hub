The Hurricane That Destroyed Spain’s First Pensacola Colony

September 19, 1559 — Pensacola Bay, Florida

By Wm. E. McMullen II

On the night of September 19, 1559, the future of Spanish Florida changed in a matter of hours.

Only weeks earlier, Tristán de Luna y Arellano had sailed into Pensacola Bay at the head of one of Spain’s most ambitious colonizing expeditions in North America. His fleet carried approximately 1,500 people — soldiers, civilian colonists, missionaries, Africans, and Indigenous people from New Spain — along with horses, equipment, food, and everything needed to establish a permanent Spanish settlement on Florida’s northern Gulf Coast.

The expedition had come from Veracruz, Mexico. Its first settlement, established on Pensacola Bay, was known as Santa María de Ochuse.

Spain intended it to be much more than an isolated outpost. From Pensacola, Luna’s expedition was supposed to establish a route inland toward the Native chiefdom of Coosa and ultimately help connect Spain’s Gulf Coast presence with another planned settlement at Santa Elena on the Atlantic coast.

It was an enormous undertaking.

Then came the hurricane.

The Storm Arrives

During the night of September 19–20, a powerful hurricane entered Pensacola Bay. For approximately 24 hours, violent wind and water battered Luna’s fleet.

The disaster was particularly devastating because much of the expedition’s food and supplies remained aboard the ships anchored in the bay. The colonists had not yet finished unloading them.

Seven vessels were lost. Six were driven aground and sank. Another was carried inland by the storm and deposited on land among trees near the settlement. Among the vessels destroyed was the Jesús, the expedition’s flagship.

Ships that only weeks earlier had carried an entire colony across the Gulf of Mexico were now wreckage. But the loss of the ships was only the beginning.

Food Was the Real Disaster

A ship could eventually be replaced. Food could not.

The colonists had arrived in a new land with approximately 1,500 people to feed. Their survival depended heavily upon the provisions transported aboard the fleet. When the hurricane destroyed the ships, it also destroyed or scattered much of that food.

Suddenly, an expedition designed to establish a permanent colony became a struggle simply to keep its people alive.

Luna's colonists began looking inland for food and assistance. Groups traveled northward into what is now Alabama, eventually occupying the abandoned Native settlement of Nanipacana along the Alabama River. Others traveled still farther toward Coosa.

Relief supplies arrived from New Spain, but the colony never fully recovered from the catastrophe. What had begun as an ambitious attempt to establish Spain permanently on the northern Gulf Coast slowly unraveled. By 1561, the settlement had been abandoned.

Six Years Before St. Augustine

The Luna expedition is sometimes overshadowed by another famous date in Florida history: 1565 — the founding of St. Augustine.

But Luna arrived at Pensacola in 1559, six years earlier.

His colony did not survive permanently, so St. Augustine retains its distinction as the oldest continuously occupied European-established settlement in what is now the continental United States.

Pensacola's Luna settlement represents something different — and historically remarkable. Archaeological research identifies it as the earliest multi-year European colonial settlement yet archaeologically documented in the United States.

Had the hurricane not destroyed Luna's fleet and supplies, the history of Spanish settlement in Florida might have unfolded very differently. We cannot know what would have happened. What we do know is that on September 19, 1559, nature dealt Spain's Pensacola colony a blow from which it never recovered.

The Ships Were Not Gone Forever

For more than four centuries, most physical evidence of Luna's disaster remained beneath Pensacola Bay. Then archaeology began bringing the story back.

In 1992, archaeologists discovered what became known as Emanuel Point I, a sixteenth-century Spanish shipwreck associated with Luna's fleet. A second wreck, Emanuel Point II, was discovered in 2006. Then, in 2016, University of West Florida archaeologists announced the discovery of Emanuel Point III.

Three ships from Luna's lost fleet had been found.

Their remains — timbers, fasteners, pottery, bricks, food remains, and other artifacts — provide a physical connection to the men and women who sailed into Pensacola Bay in 1559 expecting to build a new Spanish colony.

Even more remarkably, archaeologists found the colony itself. In 2015, Pensacola resident Tom Garner discovered sixteenth-century Spanish and Native American artifacts on private property overlooking the bay. Subsequent archaeological investigation by the University of West Florida confirmed an extensive settlement site dating to the Luna expedition.

The colony and its wrecked ships had been reunited archaeologically more than 450 years after the hurricane scattered them.

Florida and the Hurricane

There is another reason the Luna story feels distinctly Floridian. Hurricanes have shaped Florida history repeatedly.

They have destroyed towns, altered coastlines, wrecked ships, changed agriculture, redirected development, and influenced where Floridians could safely build and rebuild. But few storms can claim a consequence quite like the hurricane of September 19, 1559.

Spain arrived at Pensacola with ships, colonists, soldiers, supplies, and plans for a permanent foothold on the Gulf Coast. Twenty-four hours of wind and water changed those plans.

The colony struggled for nearly two more years, but it never recovered. Today, beneath the waters and neighborhoods of modern Pensacola, archaeology preserves pieces of the story.

On this day in Florida history — September 19, 1559 — a hurricane struck Pensacola Bay and helped determine where Spain’s first lasting chapter in Florida would, and would not, be written.
