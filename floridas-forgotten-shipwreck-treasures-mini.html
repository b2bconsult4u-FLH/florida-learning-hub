Florida’s Forgotten Shipwreck Treasures

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

Nearly Five Centuries of Storms, Shipwrecks, and Lost Treasure

Florida’s coast was repeatedly where Spain’s convoy system met its limits.

Florida’s coastline has claimed a very large number of ships over nearly five hundred years. Spanish vessels carrying New World silver, French and English merchantmen, Civil War-era steamers, schooners and later steel ships have all gone down in Florida waters, and the state’s reefs, shoals, currents and hurricanes have taken a steady share of the traffic. This overview introduces the layered history of what those waters swallowed, and of the people who have spent centuries trying to bring it back.

Why Florida’s Waters Took So Many Ships

The Florida Keys are fringed by the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States, an ecological treasure and one of the most hazardous stretches of water a sailing ship could meet. The Straits of Florida lie in the path of the Gulf Stream, a current strong enough to carry a vessel toward the reefs before its crew understood its position. Above all loomed hurricane season, when Spanish fleets sailed home. During the sixteenth century the crown organized its American trade into armed convoys with escort warships, partly to guard against privateers. That system sustained transatlantic trade for generations, but it could not defeat a hurricane.

Emanuel Point

Florida’s documented shipwreck history reaches back before any treasure fleet disaster. In 1559 Tristán de Luna y Arellano led a Spanish expedition to settle Pensacola Bay. According to the University of West Florida, a hurricane struck about five weeks after the expedition arrived and sank several ships, and the colony did not last. The first of the wrecks, Emanuel Point I, was found in 1992 by a state team led by Roger Smith; University of West Florida archaeologists later located Emanuel Point II (2006) and III (2016). The Luna expedition predates the 1622 loss of the Tierra Firme fleet by about sixty years.

1622: The Atocha and the Margarita

In September 1622 the Tierra Firme fleet, sailing home to Spain, ran into a hurricane in the Florida Keys. Sources give its size as 27 or 28 ships and the number lost as seven or eight. The Nuestra Señora de Atocha and the Santa Margarita were among those lost, carrying silver, gold, emeralds and other cargo. One educational source reports about 380 lives lost on those two ships, while the Mel Fisher Maritime Museum describes the toll across the fleet only as hundreds. Modern dollar valuations vary and are not given here. Mel Fisher’s company found the Atocha’s main cargo site in 1985; its story is told in FLH-0129.

1715 and 1733: Two Disasters, Two Coasts

In late July 1715 a hurricane destroyed a Spanish fleet off Florida’s east coast, in the area later called the Treasure Coast (FLH-0125). Less than two decades later, a second fleet met disaster in the Keys. According to Indiana University’s Center for Underwater Science, a fleet under Captain-General Rodrigo de Torres left Havana on July 13, 1733, and a hurricane struck within a day or two (accounts differ on July 14 or 15). Sources differ on whether 21 or 22 ships were involved but agree that nearly all were lost, scattered along a stretch of the Keys that accounts measure at about thirty to about eighty miles. Spain recovered part of the cargo in the 1730s, and educational sources report that wrecks were burned to the waterline to deter looters.

The San Pedro, one of the lost 1733 ships, lies in shallow water near Indian Key and is now a State of Florida Underwater Archaeological Preserve. According to NOAA, it was heavily salvaged by treasure hunters before state and federal laws protected it.

Wrecking: Florida’s Salvage Trade

After Florida became a United States territory in 1821, salvaging wrecked cargo became a major business centered on the Keys. Wreckers brought salvaged goods and ships to Key West, where courts and auctions settled the awards. By custom, saving lives came before saving cargo. Wrecking faded as navigation, lighthouses and ship design improved.

What Came After

Modern rediscovery followed: Kip Wagner’s work on the 1715 sites (FLH-0128) and Mel Fisher’s Atocha find (FLH-0129). The Florida Department of State’s Museums in the Sea program lists a dozen shipwrecks as Underwater Archaeological Preserves, from the San Pedro and Urca de Lima to steamships and vessels sunk as artificial reefs.

Quick Facts

Earliest wreck discussed

Emanuel Point I, from the 1559 Luna expedition on Pensacola Bay (found 1992)

Hazards

Coral reefs, shoals, the Gulf Stream and hurricanes

Spain’s response

Armed convoys with escort warships in the sixteenth century

1622

Tierra Firme fleet hurricane in the Keys; counts of ships and dead vary

1715

Hurricane off the east coast; see FLH-0125

1733

Fleet left Havana July 13; hurricane next day; 21 or 22 ships, nearly all lost

Wrecking

Regulated salvage trade centered on Key West after 1821

Modern era

Wagner on the 1715 sites; Fisher’s Atocha find in 1985

Protected sites

State preserves, including the San Pedro near Indian Key

Did You Know?

* A Pensacola Bay wreck from 1559 predates the Atocha by about sixty years.

* Sources disagree on how many ships the 1733 hurricane destroyed: 21 or 22.

* Under wrecking custom, saving lives came before saving cargo.

Vocabulary

Wrecker: a person who salvaged cargo and ships from vessels wrecked on reefs.

Convoy: a group of ships sailing together under armed escort.

Underwater Archaeological Preserve: a state-designated shipwreck site protected and open to visitors.
