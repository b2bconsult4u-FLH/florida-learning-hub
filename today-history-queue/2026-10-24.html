Deep Space 1 Leaves Cape Canaveral

1998 — A Florida launch sent a technology-testing spacecraft toward a new way of navigating and traveling through space.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

A Launch With an Engineering Assignment

On October 24, 1998, a Delta II rocket launched Deep Space 1 from Cape Canaveral’s Launch Complex 17A. The spacecraft’s main assignment was to demonstrate twelve technologies for future missions. Its Florida departure was the beginning of an experiment in how exploration could be done, rather than simply a trip to one destination. NASA’s mission record identifies ion propulsion and autonomous navigation among its central tests.[1][2]

A Small Push Over a Long Time

An ion engine uses electrical energy to accelerate charged particles. Deep Space 1’s system used xenon. It produced a gentle thrust that could operate for extended periods, gradually changing the spacecraft’s motion. That is a different task from lifting a rocket off a launch pad. The Delta II carried the spacecraft into space; the experimental electric propulsion system was tested after launch. Confusing those stages would turn a remarkable technology into the wrong kind of engine.[1][3]

A Machine That Could Help Navigate

Deep Space 1 also tested software intended to reduce dependence on continuous instructions from Earth. Autonomous optical navigation used observations to help determine the spacecraft’s course. For missions far from home, the ability to perform some tasks aboard the spacecraft offered an important practical advantage. The purpose of a demonstration flight was to expose new systems to real operating conditions before later missions relied on them.[1][2]

Science Beyond the Original Tests

The mission encountered asteroid Braille in 1999 and comet Borrelly during its extended mission in September 2001. Those encounters supplied scientific information as well as opportunities to operate the spacecraft. NASA credits the mission with helping establish ion propulsion for later exploration, including Dawn. Deep Space 1 was shut down in December 2001 after a mission that had extended beyond its original technology-testing phase.[1][3]

Florida as a Starting Point

A launch photograph captures only a brief part of a spacecraft’s working life. The research, testing, navigation, and later encounters stretch far beyond the moment of liftoff. October 24 links that larger story to Florida’s launch coast. It is a useful reminder that some of the most consequential departures from Cape Canaveral carried machines designed to make the next generation of journeys possible.
