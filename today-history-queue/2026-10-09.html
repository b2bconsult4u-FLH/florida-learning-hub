A Night Attack Brings War to Santa Rosa Island

October 9, 1861 — Confederate troops attacked a Union camp near Fort Pickens, but the battle did not dislodge the federal garrison.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

Across Pensacola Bay

Before dawn on October 9, 1861, Confederate troops attacked Union soldiers on Santa Rosa Island near Pensacola. The Battle of Santa Rosa Island brought the Civil War into a landscape of dunes, woods and wetlands outside Fort Pickens.

The National Park Service distinguishes the preparation from the fighting. Confederate forces assembled on the night of October 8, but the attack on Union pickets began about 3:30 a.m. on October 9. Dating the battle to the ninth keeps those events in their proper order.

The Camp and the Fort

Confederate troops under Richard H. Anderson overran Camp Brown, where soldiers of the 6th New York were stationed. The attackers burned the camp. Union reinforcements came from Fort Pickens and fought the Confederate force before it withdrew toward its landing place.

The camp and the fort were not the same target or structure. Capturing an exposed camp did not mean Fort Pickens had fallen. The Museum of Florida History records the Confederate landing and assault, while Florida Memory confirms that the fort remained in Union hands throughout the war.

Confusion in the Dark

The National Park Service describes the terrain breaking large formations into smaller groups. Darkness, dunes and vegetation complicated the fighting and separated soldiers from their officers. These details help explain why the engagement should not be imagined as two tidy lines advancing across an open field.

Both sides claimed victory afterward. A useful measure of the result is what changed on the ground: neither army was driven out of the Pensacola region by this battle, and the federal position at Fort Pickens survived.

Why It Matters

Florida's Civil War included contested coastal positions as well as inland campaigns. Santa Rosa Island shows how control of a bay and a fort could matter even when a nighttime attack failed to produce a decisive strategic change.

The battlefield is now preserved within Gulf Islands National Seashore. Its history gives a familiar coastal landscape another dimension. Behind the beaches lies a story of military decisions, frightened soldiers and families whose losses were much greater than the territory won or lost.
