A Treaty Defines Spanish Florida’s Northern Boundary

1795 — The Treaty of San Lorenzo settled a disputed frontier and changed access to the Mississippi River.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

An Agreement Signed in Spain

On October 27, 1795, Thomas Pinckney and Manuel de Godoy signed the Treaty of San Lorenzo, often called Pinckney’s Treaty. The agreement addressed boundaries between the United States and Spanish possessions and access to the Mississippi River. For Florida history, it is a reminder that the shape of the peninsula’s colonial frontier was influenced by negotiations far across the Atlantic.[1][2]

Drawing a Boundary in Words

Article II described the southern boundary of the United States against Spain’s East and West Florida. It began at the Mississippi River on the thirty-first parallel, ran east to the Apalachicola-Chattahoochee river system, and continued by rivers and a connecting line toward the Atlantic. This was a written description that still required work on the ground. Article III called for commissioners and surveyors to mark the boundary and keep records of their proceedings.[2]

The River Was Part of the Bargain

The treaty also opened Mississippi navigation to American citizens and allowed the deposit of goods at New Orleans without the duties that had been a major point of dispute. The Office of the Historian explains why western and southern Americans welcomed the agreement: river access and a settled frontier made expansion and trade more attractive. Spain’s changing position in European wars helped create the opportunity for Pinckney’s negotiation.[1]

The People Outside the Negotiating Room

The agreement was a diplomatic success for the United States, but that description does not mean every affected community benefited. The State Department’s historical account notes that Spain’s retreat from guarantees to Native communities weakened their ability to resist encroachment. A boundary negotiated between empires and republics could reshape the lives of people who had not been parties to its terms. A complete frontier history must leave room for those consequences.[1]

Signature Was Not Completion

October 27 is the signature date. Ratification and implementation followed in 1796 and afterward. A line in a treaty, a marked boundary, and a functioning political border are related but different things. This anniversary captures the written agreement at the start of that process. It also shows why Florida’s past cannot be understood only by looking within the state’s present outline: its earlier boundaries belonged to a much wider struggle over land, waterways, and power.[2]
