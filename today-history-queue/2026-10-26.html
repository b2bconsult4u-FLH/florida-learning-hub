A National Park Protects the Dry Tortugas

1992 — Congress redesignated an already protected island fortress and its surrounding waters as Dry Tortugas National Park.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

A New Designation for an Older Place

On October 26, 1992, Congress redesignated Fort Jefferson National Monument as Dry Tortugas National Park. Federal protection had begun much earlier: President Franklin D. Roosevelt established the monument in 1935. The 1992 anniversary therefore marks a change in designation and purpose, not the first discovery or protection of the islands. The National Park Service’s history sets those separate milestones alongside one another.[1]

Where Routes and Reefs Meet

The Dry Tortugas lie about seventy miles west of Key West. Their position near important shipping routes helps explain the presence of Fort Jefferson, lighthouses, and shipwrecks. The islands’ surrounding reefs made navigation hazardous even as the location made them strategically attractive. A fortress and a marine park can appear to tell different stories; here they occupy the same landscape because both stories begin with the relationship between land and water.[1]

The Fortress and Its Setting

Construction of Fort Jefferson began in the nineteenth century, but the great masonry structure was never completed. The Park Service describes difficulties with supplies, subsidence, and the burden additional construction would place on the fort and its water-storage system. Preserving the fort means caring for a structure exposed to the same water, weather, and isolation that shaped its working life. Its walls are an important part of the park, but they are not the whole of it.[1]

What Congress Asked the Park to Protect

The enabling legislation identifies natural, historic, scenic, marine, and scientific values. Its management purposes include protecting coral reef communities, wildlife, the islands’ environment, Fort Jefferson, and submerged cultural resources. Shipwrecks belong within that framework alongside living habitats. The law also provides for scientific research consistent with protection. This wider mandate helps explain why the name Dry Tortugas National Park conveys more than a monument centered on one building.[2]

An Anniversary About Stewardship

A national park designation is a decision about what should survive for people who have not yet arrived. At the Dry Tortugas, that responsibility crosses the shoreline: the visible fort and islands share a protected setting with resources underwater. October 26 offers a way to read the park as a connected place, where military history, navigation, marine life, and preservation must be understood together.
