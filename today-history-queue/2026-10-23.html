Olustee Becomes Florida’s First State Historic Site

1912 — A memorial dedication at Olustee marked a milestone in Florida’s public preservation of history.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

Two Dates, Two Different Events

On October 23, 1912, veterans and dignitaries gathered at Olustee to dedicate a memorial. Florida State Parks identifies the occasion with the state’s first historic site. The battle being commemorated had taken place on February 20, 1864. The October anniversary belongs to the work of remembrance nearly half a century later, not to the fighting itself.[1][2]

A Battlefield Enters Public Memory

Preserving a place gives later generations a location in which to encounter the past. A monument adds another layer: it records what its sponsors chose to honor and how they wished that event to be understood. The Olustee memorial arose from Confederate commemoration. Its presence is evidence of the early twentieth century’s memory of the war as well as a marker pointing back to the nineteenth-century battle.[1]

Who Fought at Olustee?

The battle was Florida’s largest Civil War engagement. The Union force included Black soldiers, among them the 54th Massachusetts and the 8th United States Colored Troops. Florida’s Department of Environmental Protection has highlighted the participation of three African American regiments. Their service belongs in the story even when a commemorative photograph or Confederate monument does not represent them. Understanding a battlefield requires looking beyond the people most visible in its memorial landscape.[1][3]

Reading the Dedication Record

The State Archives preserves photographs of survivors gathered for the dedication. These are records of men looking back on an experience, not images of combat. Their age, the ceremony, and the existence of a monument all belong to the later moment. Photographs of a reunion can help a researcher ask who attended and who was recognized. They cannot, by themselves, establish the experiences of every regiment or everyone affected by the war.[2]

Preservation and a Fuller Account

Historic places are valuable because the questions asked of them can grow. The fact that a site began with a particular commemorative purpose need not limit every future explanation. October 23 invites readers to consider both the achievement of preserving a battlefield and the responsibility to tell its history broadly. The battle, the memorial, and the interpretation of both are related subjects, each with its own evidence and date.
