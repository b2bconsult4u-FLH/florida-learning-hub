Spain's Rum Ban Reaches Colonial Florida

September 30, 1714 — A royal order against cane liquor begins a long journey to St. Augustine.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

An archival record dated September 30, 1714, carried a sweeping royal command: Spanish officials were not to permit the manufacture or sale of liquor distilled from sugar cane. The order applied across Spain's American possessions. A surviving Florida-related copy shows how that command eventually became a local question in St. Augustine.

What the Crown Tried to Stop

The drink was called aguardiente de caña—literally, burning water made from cane. Distillers could turn sugar byproducts into a strong, inexpensive liquor. That local product competed with wine and brandy shipped through Spain's regulated Atlantic trade and was difficult for royal authorities to tax and control.

The crown had issued prohibitions before. The September 30 order repeated the ban and directed viceroys, governors, courts and other officials in New Spain and Peru to suppress production and both public and private sales. On paper, the command reached an enormous empire. In practice, enforcement depended on distant officials and local conditions.

From Madrid to St. Augustine

Spanish Florida was a remote military colony whose capital depended heavily on supplies and money routed through Havana and Mexico. A decree did not arrive instantly. Copies moved by ship and official correspondence, then had to be proclaimed and interpreted by colonial authorities.

The University of Florida's John H. Hann Papers preserve a record describing the prohibition's implementation in St. Augustine in September 1715 in response to the king's September 30, 1714, orders. The year between the dated order and Florida's response illustrates the slow machinery of eighteenth-century government.

A Law Meets a Frontier Economy

St. Augustine was not a plantation center like parts of the Caribbean, but it was connected to those markets. Soldiers, sailors, laborers, merchants and officials consumed imported goods, and local trade often exceeded what imperial rules formally allowed. Alcohol could be a commodity, a ration, a medicine and a source of disorder—all at once.

That made prohibition more complicated than simply announcing a ban. Officials had to identify producers and sellers, decide how aggressively to police them and balance royal policy against the daily needs of a small garrison town. The record is a window into the constant negotiation between orders written at court and life at the edge of empire.

Why It Matters

This unusual document makes colonial Florida tangible. Imperial history was not only forts, battles and governors. It also reached into workshops, taverns, kitchens and marketplaces, regulating what people could make, sell and drink.

The story also explains why historians pay attention to dates on administrative records. September 30 marks the order preserved in the archival chain, while St. Augustine's implementation came later. That delay reveals how information traveled and why royal power, although vast on paper, was never automatic on the ground.
