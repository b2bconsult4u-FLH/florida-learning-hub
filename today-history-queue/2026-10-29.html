John Glenn Returns to Orbit from Florida at 77

1998 — Discovery’s STS-95 launch joined a famous return to space with an international crew’s scientific work.

By Wm. E. McMullen II

A Second Departure From Florida

On October 29, 1998, space shuttle Discovery lifted off from Kennedy Space Center’s Launch Pad 39B. Aboard was John Glenn, returning to space more than thirty-six years after his Friendship 7 flight made him the first American to orbit Earth. At seventy-seven, Glenn became the oldest person to reach orbit at that time. The distinction concerns an orbital mission, rather than a brief suborbital journey.[1][2]

Seven People, One Mission

Glenn’s presence drew extraordinary attention, but STS-95 carried a seven-person crew. Curtis Brown commanded the mission and Steven Lindsey served as pilot. Mission specialists were Scott Parazynski, Stephen Robinson, and Pedro Duque; payload specialists were Chiaki Mukai and Glenn. Duque represented the European Space Agency, while Mukai came from Japan’s space agency. Listing the crew keeps a celebrated individual’s return within the shared work that made it possible.[1][2]

Research Beyond the Headlines

The mission carried more than eighty experiments. Research examined similarities between changes associated with aging and some effects of weightlessness, including sleep, balance, and bone and muscle loss. Other work included materials and biological studies. The crew also deployed and retrieved the Spartan solar-observation spacecraft. NASA’s accounts describe a working laboratory and multiple research objectives, not simply an honorary ride for a famous former astronaut.[1][2]

Comparing Two Eras

Glenn’s 1962 flight and the 1998 shuttle mission offer a striking comparison. The earlier journey was a solo Mercury flight; Discovery carried an international team and a much larger scientific workload. Both began from Florida, but they belonged to different stages of human spaceflight. The familiar face returning to orbit made that change easier to recognize without making the two missions equivalent.[1]

The Launch and What Followed

Discovery returned to Kennedy Space Center on November 7 after its research mission. October 29 remains the date of departure, a moment when a long personal ambition met the practical demands of a scientific flight. For Florida history, the anniversary connects the state’s launch facilities to a story stretching across generations. For the history of exploration, it also invites attention to the whole crew, the ground teams, and the experiments behind an unforgettable headline.[2]
