How Florida Learned to Respond · FLH-0018

The Great Florida Evacuations

Written by Wm. E. McMullen II · Florida History Mini

How a State Built to Drive Its Way to Safety Learned to Move Millions at Once

On the morning of October 4, 1995, Gulf Coast Florida residents woke to news that should never have surprised anyone, and yet did. The hurricane they had gone to bed watching as a Category 2 storm with 100 mile-per-hour winds had, by four that morning, strengthened to 135 miles per hour. By ten, it carried sustained winds of 150, just shy of Category 5 intensity. Thousands of Panhandle residents who had been content to wait and see now tried to leave all at once, and Interstate 65 north of Pensacola became a parking lot, narrowed to a single lane by highway construction.

A Lesson as Old as the Keys

Florida’s evacuation story did not begin with Opal. Roughly half of the Middle and Upper Keys residents left ahead of the Labor Day Hurricane of 1935, a real improvement in preparedness for its era, even as the rescue train sent for the rest arrived at the moment the surge struck. The pattern would repeat for ninety years: the question was never only whether people would leave, but whether the roads could carry them away faster than the storm approached.

For most of the twentieth century, evacuation planning received little dedicated attention, even as the state’s population and highway network grew. Opal forced the issue.

Opal’s Gridlock

Opal’s rapid intensification gave Panhandle residents almost no margin for an orderly departure. Landfall came with sustained winds of 115 miles per hour, but the earlier, more alarming forecast had already set the evacuation in motion. Traffic stopped on I-65, on U.S. 231 and U.S. 331, the main escape routes from Fort Walton Beach and Panama City, and on U.S. 29, the main road north out of the Pensacola area, home to roughly 280,000 people.

For two hours, state police opened the southbound lanes of U.S. 231 and 331 to northbound traffic, an improvised version of the contraflow idea that would occupy Florida planners for years without ever becoming state policy. By midday, Governor Lawton Chiles issued a blunt warning: anyone who had not already left should stay home or find shelter nearby, because the roads themselves had become a hazard.

A Plan, and a Question That Lingered

Florida designed its first Regional Hurricane Evacuation Plan in 1996 to coordinate departures across its most populous coastal regions instead of leaving counties to manage traffic alone. The plan addressed coordination and timing, but not the physical shortage of outbound lanes.

The proposed fix, contraflow, temporarily converts inbound lanes to carry outbound traffic. A 2008 University of South Florida doctoral dissertation noted that Florida had spent more time and resources planning contraflow facilities than any other state, yet had not implemented it on a single highway.

Floyd and the National Pattern

Florida was not alone. In 1999, Hurricane Floyd prompted what FEMA described at the time as the largest evacuation in U.S. history, roughly three million people across Florida, Georgia and the Carolinas, and many were stranded in the same kind of gridlock Opal had produced four years earlier. Floyd became a cautionary reference for planners across the Southeast: a region able to order millions to leave needs roads able to carry them.

The Shoulder, Not the Opposing Lane

Florida’s practical answer was simpler than full contraflow: Emergency Shoulder Use. Instead of reversing a highway, which demands heavy law enforcement coordination, median crossovers and signage, the state opened the paved shoulder on key evacuation corridors to ordinary travel during a declared emergency. All motorists except large trucks, buses and trailers could use the inside shoulder on six-lane roads or the outside shoulder on four-lane roads, entering and exiting only at marked points.

Irma: The Largest Evacuation

The system’s first true test came with Hurricane Irma in September 2017. With the entire peninsula inside the National Hurricane Center’s cone of uncertainty, evacuees on both coasts found little option but to go north. About 6.5 million people were placed under evacuation orders, the largest evacuation in state history, with twenty-three counties under mandatory orders and forty-four more under voluntary orders. Florida Department of Transportation counts showed roughly 4,000 vehicles per hour northbound on I-75 at Lake City, against a normal flow near 1,000.

Florida opened the left shoulder of northbound I-75 from Wildwood to the Georgia line beginning September 8, and briefly on I-4 eastbound between Tampa and State Road 429 the next day.

When the Fuel Ran Short

Irma exposed a different weakness: fuel. Port closures beginning September 8 disrupted the seaborne deliveries that supply most of the state’s gasoline just as demand spiked. Stations had hours-long lines, and the Florida Highway Patrol began escorting fuel trucks. Florida’s alternate-power requirements for fuel facilities were already in law before Irma. Section 526.143 was created in 2006 and covers specified service stations and fuel distribution facilities; it includes a 72-hour alternate-power capability for terminals and wholesalers. The 2017 shortages showed that backup electricity alone could not prevent interruptions to the wider fuel supply chain.

Did You Know?

Opal’s two-hour gridlock produced an improvised contraflow on U.S. 231 and 331, years before Florida studied the idea formally.

Emergency Shoulder Use adds outbound capacity without reversing opposing highway lanes.

Irma’s evacuation was hampered by a fuel shortage as well as traffic.

Vocabulary

Contraflow: temporarily reversing inbound lanes to carry outbound evacuation traffic.

Emergency Shoulder Use: opening a highway’s paved shoulder to ordinary traffic during a declared emergency.

Cone of uncertainty: the National Hurricane Center’s forecast area showing where a storm’s center may travel.

Discussion Questions

Why did Florida choose Emergency Shoulder Use over full contraflow?

How did Opal in 1995 and Irma in 2017 differ in what went wrong on the roads?
